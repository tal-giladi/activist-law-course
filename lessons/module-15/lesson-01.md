# 15.1 · The Basics of International Humanitarian Law

International humanitarian law (IHL), also called the law of armed conflict, sets rules for how wars are fought, whoever started them and whoever is right. In this lesson you learn when IHL applies, which of its rules bind Israel as treaty law and which only as custom, and the three rules that come up in almost every public argument about an attack: distinction, proportionality and precautions. You finish by building a one-page rules card you will use for the rest of the module.

> [!NOTE]
> This module deals with an active, painful conflict. The course takes no position on it. It teaches the rules, shows who says what (Israel's government, Israeli courts, UN bodies, the ICJ, NGOs and scholars from different sides) and attributes every contested claim. Real-world fieldwork in conflict zones is out of scope: everything here is desk work from public sources.

## Why it matters

After every round of fighting, Israelis and Palestinians, politicians, journalists and NGOs argue in the language of IHL: "a war crime", "a legitimate target", "disproportionate", "human shields", "they were warned". Most of these claims are made without naming the rule, the facts the rule needs, or who is bound by it. If you know the basic rules in their actual wording, you can read an IDF statement, an NGO report or a UN document and see exactly what it claims and what it would have to prove. That makes you harder to mislead and more credible, whichever cause you support.

## The law

### When IHL applies

> In addition to the provisions which shall be implemented in peace-time, the present Convention shall apply to all cases of declared war or of any other armed conflict which may arise between two or more of the High Contracting Parties, even if the state of war is not recognized by one of them.

*Gist:* the Geneva Conventions apply in full to armed conflicts between states (international armed conflicts), whether or not war is declared (Geneva Convention IV (GC IV), art. 2(1)).

> In the case of armed conflict not of an international character occurring in the territory of one of the High Contracting Parties, each Party to the conflict shall be bound to apply, as a minimum, the following provisions:

*Gist:* in a non-international armed conflict, every party, including an armed group, is bound by the minimum rules of Common Article 3 (humane treatment; no murder, torture, hostage-taking or trials without a regular court) (GC IV, art. 3).

Full text: [GC IV (Avalon Project)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva07.asp); [ICRC Geneva Conventions page](https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/geneva-conventions-and-their-commentaries).

### The three core conduct-of-hostilities rules (customary wording)

> Rule 1. The parties to the conflict must at all times distinguish between civilians and combatants. Attacks may only be directed against combatants. Attacks must not be directed against civilians.

*Gist:* distinction between people (ICRC customary Rule 1, applies in IAC and NIAC). Rule 6 adds that civilians lose protection only "unless and for such time as they take a direct part in hostilities".

> Rule 8. In so far as objects are concerned, military objectives are limited to those objects which by their nature, location, purpose or use make an effective contribution to military action and whose partial or total destruction, capture or neutralization, in the circumstances ruling at the time, offers a definite military advantage.

*Gist:* a building, vehicle or site is a lawful target only if both parts of this test are met at the time (Rule 8; Rule 7 sets distinction for objects).

> Rule 14. Launching an attack which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated, is prohibited.

*Gist:* proportionality compares *expected* civilian harm with *anticipated* military advantage, at the time of the decision (Rule 14).

> Rule 15. In the conduct of military operations, constant care must be taken to spare the civilian population, civilians and civilian objects. All feasible precautions must be taken to avoid, and in any event to minimize, incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians and damage to civilian objects.

*Gist:* the attacker must take all *feasible* precautions (Rule 15), including verifying targets (Rule 16), choosing means and methods that reduce harm (Rule 17) and giving "effective advance warning of attacks which may affect the civilian population, unless circumstances do not permit" (Rule 20). The defender must also take feasible precautions to protect civilians under its control (Rule 22), and the use of human shields is prohibited (Rule 97).

Source: the list of rules annexed to [Henckaerts, IRRC 857 (2005)](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/external/doc/en/assets/files/other/icrc_002_0860.pdf); each rule with its practice is in the [ICRC Customary IHL database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl).

## How it works in practice

### Which rules bind Israel, and how

| Instrument | Israel's status | How it binds Israel |
|---|---|---|
| Geneva Conventions I–IV (1949) | Party since 1951 | As treaties (internationally) |
| Additional Protocol I (1977), on international conflicts | Not a party (check the [ICRC states-parties list](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/state-parties)) | Only those rules that reflect custom |
| Hague Convention IV and Regulations (1907) | Not a party | Israel "views the Convention as reflecting customary international law" (Israel's 2015 report, n. 397); the Supreme Court treats the Regulations as custom (lesson [13.5](../module-13/lesson-05.md)) |
| Customary IHL | Binds every state | Israel's government says it "abides by all applicable rules of customary international law, including those embodied in conventions to which it is not a party (such as the 1977 Additional Protocols)" ([MFA, Key Legal Aspects, 2023](https://israelihl.mfa.gov.il/sites/default/files/2025-08/4%20-%20English_Documents_Hamas-Israel%20Conflict%202023%20-%20Some%20Factual%20and%20Legal%20Aspects%20-%20Israel%20Ministry%20of%20Foreign%20Affairs%20%282%20NOV%202023%29.pdf)) |

This is why careful writers quote the ICRC customary rule, or an Israeli ruling that recognised a rule as custom, rather than "Article 51 of Additional Protocol I" alone. The ICRC study itself is an authoritative but non-binding statement of what custom is; states, Israel included, do not accept every rule and commentary in it.

### Classifying the conflict: who says what

Classification decides which rule-set applies. It is contested for the Israeli–Palestinian conflict:

- **The Israeli Supreme Court** in *Targeted Killings* (2006, paras 18–21) treated the conflict between Israel and armed groups in the territories as governed by the law of international armed conflict.
- **Israel's government** wrote in November 2023 that the classification of the conflict with armed groups in Gaza (IAC or NIAC) "ha[s] been a matter of debate", and that "in any event, in practice Israel conducts its present military operations in accordance with the rules of LOAC governing both international and non-international armed conflicts".
- **The ICRC study** marks most conduct-of-hostilities rules "[IAC/NIAC]": distinction, proportionality and precautions apply in both. That is why, for those three rules, the classification debate usually changes less than people think.

### Two questions you must never mix

- **Jus ad bellum:** was the resort to force lawful (self-defence, Security Council authorisation)?
- **Jus in bello (IHL):** was *this attack* lawful under distinction, proportionality and precautions?

IHL applies equally to all parties, whatever the answer to the first question. "They started it" does not make an attack lawful, and "it was self-defence" does not answer whether a given strike was proportionate. Keep the two in separate paragraphs.

### How each rule is tested

| Rule | The question | Facts you need | Typical dispute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distinction | Was the target a combatant, a civilian taking a direct part in hostilities, or a military objective? | What the target was and how it was used at the time | Dual-use objects; civilians who help armed groups indirectly |
| Proportionality | Was the *expected* civilian harm excessive compared with the *anticipated* concrete and direct military advantage? | What the attacker knew or should have known when deciding | Hindsight vs the commander's view at the time |
| Precautions | Did the attacker do everything *feasible* to verify, to choose less harmful means, and to warn? | Options available, time, intelligence, warnings given | What was "feasible" in the circumstances |

Israel's government describes proportionality as "based on the military commander's judgement at the time of the attack, not on hindsight: the test is conduct-oriented, not result-oriented" (MFA, 2023). Many NGOs and UN bodies agree that the test is prospective but argue that patterns of outcomes can be evidence of what was expected. You will meet this debate again in [15.5](lesson-05.md).

## Worked example

A residents' association in a fictional border town, Ein Hadas, is hit by rockets fired by an organised armed group from a neighbouring area. Its chair, Miriam, wants to write to a foreign parliament. At the same time, Rami, a volunteer in a human-rights group, is asked about an air strike on a building in that neighbouring area. Both want to use IHL correctly.

**Step 1 — Does IHL apply?** There are sustained hostilities between a state and an organised armed group: there is an armed conflict, so IHL applies. Both note that its classification may be debated and that the three rules they need apply in both IAC and NIAC (Rules 1, 14, 15 are marked IAC/NIAC).

**Step 2 — Which rules, from which source?** Both quote the customary rules, not AP I alone, because Israel is not a party to AP I.

**Step 3 — Miriam's letter.** Rockets aimed at a town with no military objective nearby raise distinction (Rule 1) and the prohibition on acts "the primary purpose of which is to spread terror among the civilian population" (Rule 2). She writes the facts she can prove (dates, impact sites, absence of military sites nearby, sources) and quotes the rules. She does not need to settle who started the conflict.

**Step 4 — Rami's answer.** He lists what he would need before saying anything about the strike: what the building was used for (distinction, Rule 8), what the attacker expected about civilians present and what advantage it anticipated (proportionality, Rule 14), and whether there was a warning and other feasible precautions (Rules 15–20). He writes: "We do not yet have the facts to assess this under IHL. Here is what each rule requires." He also notes the armed group's own duties (Rules 22, 97).

**What both show.** Same rules, same structure, different side. Neither writes a conclusion the facts do not support.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The [ICRC Customary IHL database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl) and the [Avalon text of GC IV](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva07.asp).
- About 90 minutes: 30 minutes on the assigned e-learning module, 60 minutes to build your rules card.

### Steps

1. Open the customary database and find Rules 1, 6, 7, 8, 14, 15, 16, 17, 20, 22 and 97. For each, copy the rule text exactly and its marker ([IAC/NIAC] or [IAC]).
2. For each rule, write in one line the facts someone would need to prove a breach.
3. Open the [ICRC states-parties page for AP I](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/state-parties) and confirm Israel's status. Write the date you checked.
4. Write two sentences on Israel's stated approach to custom and to the Additional Protocols, quoting the MFA's 2023 paper with its date.
5. Write one paragraph that separates jus ad bellum from jus in bello in your own words.
6. Test your card on this scenario: "An armed group fires from a schoolyard during a school holiday; the other side strikes the launcher an hour later." List, without answering, the questions each rule raises for each side.

You will paste this rules card into section 3 of the [IHL incident analysis template](../../templates/ihl-incident-analysis.md), which you use in lesson 15.5 and the module project.

Your card quotes each rule word for word with its source, lists the facts each rule needs, says which instruments bind Israel and how, and your test scenario produces questions for *both* sides, not conclusions.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calling any civilian death "a war crime" | Overclaims; IHL accepts incidental harm that is not excessive and was not intended | Name the rule and the facts that would show a breach | Use the "facts you need" column |
| Citing AP I as binding on Israel | The State answers that Israel is not a party | Cite the customary rule or an Israeli ruling recognising it | Check treaty status first |
| Mixing "who started it" with "was this attack lawful" | The argument is dismissed as political | Put jus ad bellum and jus in bello in separate paragraphs | Ask the two questions separately |
| Judging proportionality from the outcome only | Ignores the legal test, which looks at what was expected | Ask what the attacker knew or should have known | Write the commander's viewpoint question into your notes |
| Writing only one side's duties | Reads as advocacy and misses the defender's obligations | Add the armed group's or the defender's duties (Rules 22, 97) | Apply every rule to every party |

## Review

- IHL applies to every party to an armed conflict, whoever started it; jus ad bellum is a separate question.
- Israel is bound by the Geneva Conventions as treaties and by AP I and the Hague Regulations only where they reflect custom; its government says it abides by customary rules.
- Distinction, proportionality and precautions apply in international and non-international conflicts alike.
- Proportionality compares expected civilian harm with anticipated military advantage at the time of the decision.
- A careful analysis names the rule, the facts it needs and the source, and applies the rules to all parties.
