# 15.3 · The Israeli High Court on the Territories

Since 1967 Israel's Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, has heard petitions about almost every aspect of military rule in the territories: land, roads, the separation barrier, interrogations, targeted killings, Gaza's fuel and humanitarian aid, and Knesset laws about settlements. In this lesson you learn the framework the Court applies, the holding of each leading ruling, and the main critiques from different directions, and you finish by briefing one ruling and deciding who could rely on it.

> [!NOTE]
> Several of these rulings concern active hostilities and are contested. The course reports what the Court held and what its critics say, from more than one side. It takes no position on whether the Court was right.

## Why it matters

For an activist inside Israel, the High Court is the forum where international humanitarian law is actually applied and enforced: an order can be cancelled, a route moved, a policy changed. Palestinian villages, Israeli human-rights NGOs, settlers' councils and the State all litigate there, and all cite the same handful of rulings. If you know what each ruling actually decided, you can tell a petition that uses the case law from one that only quotes slogans, and you can predict how the State will answer.

## The law

### The framework, in the Court's words

> The Judea and Samaria areas are held by the State of Israel in belligerent occupation. The long arm of the state in the area is the military commander. He is not the sovereign in the territory held in belligerent occupation […]

*Gist:* the commander acts under the law of belligerent occupation (*Mara'abe*, HCJ 7957/04, 2005, para. 14, Versa English).

> The State of Israel has declared that it practices the humanitarian parts of this convention. In light of that declaration on the part of the government of Israel, we see no need to reexamine the government's position.

*Gist:* the Court applies GC IV's humanitarian provisions because the State says it does, without deciding whether the Convention formally applies (*Mara'abe*, para. 14).

> According to the principle of proportionality, the decision of an administrative body is legal only if the means used to realize the governmental objective is of proper proportion.

*Gist:* the commander's decisions are also reviewed under Israeli administrative law, using the three proportionality subtests (*Beit Sourik*, HCJ 2056/04, 2004, para. 40; lesson [03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md)).

### The ICJ in the High Court

> the Supreme Court of Israel shall give the full appropriate weight to the norms of international law, as developed and interpreted by the ICJ in its Advisory Opinion. However, the ICJ's conclusion, based upon a factual basis different than the one before us, is not res judicata, and does not obligate the Supreme Court of Israel to rule that each and every segment of the fence violates international law.

*Gist:* the Court weighs ICJ advisory opinions but decides on its own factual record, segment by segment (*Mara'abe*, para. 74).

Full English texts: [Versa — Mara'abe](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/mara%E2%80%99abe-v-prime-minister-israel); [Versa — Beit Sourik](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/beit-sourik-village-council-v-government-israel). The Hebrew originals are on the Supreme Court's website; these quotes use Versa's English translation.

## How it works in practice

### The leading rulings

| Ruling | Who petitioned | What the Court held |
|---|---|---|
| *Elon Moreh* (HCJ 390/79, 1979) | Palestinian landowners | A requisition of private land for a civilian settlement did not serve a military need under the Hague Regulations; the order was cancelled. Custom applies; GC IV art. 49 was treated as treaty law not enforceable in an Israeli court (lesson [13.5](../module-13/lesson-05.md)). |
| *Public Committee Against Torture v. Israel* (HCJ 5100/94, 1999) | Human-rights NGO and detainees | Security-service interrogators are "not authorized to employ any physical means, absent unequivocal authorization from the legislature"; the necessity defence may arise only afterwards, in criminal proceedings. |
| *Ajuri* (HCJ 7015/02, 2002) | Relatives of suspected militants | GC IV art. 78 allows assigned residence "for imperative reasons of security", but only where "there is a reasonable possibility that the person himself presents a real danger"; deterring others is not enough. |
| *Beit Sourik* (HCJ 2056/04, 30 June 2004) | Palestinian village councils | The barrier's purpose was security, not political, but several segments failed proportionality in the narrow sense because of severe harm to farmers; those orders were cancelled. |
| *Mara'abe* (HCJ 7957/04, 2005) | Palestinian villagers enclosed with Alfei Menashe | The ICJ's opinion is weighed but not binding; the enclave route failed the "least harmful means" subtest and had to be reconsidered. The commander's art. 43 authority covers everyone in the area, including Israeli residents. |
| *Targeted Killings* (HCJ 769/02, 2006) | Human-rights NGOs | Targeted killings are neither always prohibited nor always permitted under custom; civilians lose protection only while taking a direct part in hostilities; proportionality applies; each case needs prior examination and an independent investigation afterwards. |
| *Al-Bassiouni* (HCJ 9132/07, 2008) | Human-rights NGOs | No effective control of Gaza since 2005; Israel must allow what is needed for "essential humanitarian needs"; the reduced fuel and electricity supply was found to meet them and the petition was dismissed. |
| *Silwad* (HCJ 1308/17, 2020) | Palestinian municipalities and NGOs | The Settlement Regularization Law was void, 8 to 1, for violating property, dignity and equality under Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty. Justice Sohlberg, dissenting, held the purpose of regularising settlement proper. |
| *Gisha* (HCJ 2280/24, 27 Mar 2025) | Israeli human-rights NGOs | During the war, no belligerent occupation of Gaza; Israel's humanitarian duties derive from the laws of war and Israeli administrative law; the petition on humanitarian aid was dismissed. |

The *Silwad* and *Gisha* summaries rely on the Verfassungsblog, Lieber Institute and INSS accounts in this lesson's sources; the Hebrew judgments are on the Supreme Court's website and were not opened for this lesson.

### What the pattern shows

- **The Court reviews, it rarely blocks a policy outright.** It accepts the security purpose the commander states, unless shown otherwise (*Beit Sourik*, para. 45), and then examines proportionality case by case.
- **Petitioners win on specific harm.** Successful petitions show a concrete injury (a farmer cut off from land, a route with a less harmful alternative), not the illegality of the whole policy.
- **Petitioners from every side use it.** Palestinian villages win route changes; settlers' councils rely on the commander's art. 43 duty to protect them; NGOs obtain standards for interrogation and targeting; the State wins petitions on Gaza supplies and aid.

### Critiques from different directions

- **That the Court does too little.** Tamar Hostovsky Brandes (Verfassungsblog, 2020) notes that in *Silwad* the Court chose a constitutional route and "refrained from addressing the authority of the Knesset to legislate in the Occupied Territories". Ori Pomson (Lieber Institute, 2025) argues that in *Gisha* the Court did not fully engage with the ICJ's functional approach to occupation. David Kretzmer's 2012 survey reviews the Court's record on settlements, military needs and the long duration of the occupation (abstract in the sources).
- **That the Court does too much.** The Kohelet Policy Forum (Shalev, 2025) argues that the Court has used international law too broadly in reviewing government action (lesson 13.5). Justice Sohlberg's dissent in *Silwad* treated settlement in Judea and Samaria as a proper legislative purpose.
- **The State's position.** In its responses the State asks the Court to defer to military judgment on security and argues that the Court should not decide political questions such as the territories' final status.

## Worked example

Daniel volunteers with a disability-rights group. Rana works with a Palestinian farmers' cooperative. Yossi chairs a residents' committee in a community beyond the Green Line. Each asks: "Which High Court ruling helps us?"

**Rana: a new barrier gate.** A gate opens for one hour a day and farmers cannot reach their olive groves at harvest. *Beit Sourik* is the closest ruling: the Court accepted the security purpose but found that some segments harmed farmers out of proportion to the added security, and it examined gates and permits. Her letter asks the commander to extend gate hours at harvest, citing the three subtests and the less harmful alternative. She does not argue that the barrier as a whole is unlawful; the Court has rejected that framing.

**Yossi: a road without protection.** Stones and firebombs hit cars on the access road. *Mara'abe* (para. 14 and the discussion of art. 43) is his ruling: the commander's authority and duty to ensure safety extend to Israeli residents. His letter asks for a concrete security plan within a deadline, as a pre-petition letter (lesson [05.5](../module-05/lesson-05.md)).

**Daniel: a disabled detainee.** A Palestinian detainee with a disability is held in conditions his family describes as unsuitable. *Public Committee Against Torture* (1999) limits physical means in interrogation and is often cited for the principle that security bodies need clear legal authority. Daniel's group notes that the case concerns interrogation, not detention conditions, and looks for a closer source (GC IV detention provisions, Israeli prison rules) rather than stretching the ruling.

**What all three show.** Use a ruling for what it held. Each petitioner chose the closest holding, from whichever side of the debate it came.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The Versa page of one ruling from the table (choose *Beit Sourik*, *Mara'abe*, *Targeted Killings* or *Al-Bassiouni*).
- The [ruling-brief template](../../templates/ruling-brief.md) from lesson 01.6.
- About 110 minutes: 60 minutes reading, 50 minutes writing.

### Steps

1. Read the headnote and the paragraphs this lesson cites, then skim the rest for the outcome.
2. Fill in the ruling-brief template: parties, question, framework (Hague, GC IV, administrative law), holding, outcome, key paragraph quoted exactly.
3. Write one sentence on what the ruling does *not* decide (for example, *Mara'abe* does not decide the barrier's legality as a whole; *Al-Bassiouni* does not decide what Israel owes if it again controls Gaza).
4. Write one critique from each of two directions, each attributed to a named source in this lesson or another you found and opened.
5. Choose two petitioners from different causes and write, for each, one sentence on how they could rely on the ruling and one on its limits for them.
6. Add the brief to section 2 of the [IHL incident analysis template](../../templates/ihl-incident-analysis.md) under "Israeli case law".

### Template

[ruling-brief.md](../../templates/ruling-brief.md) for the brief; [ihl-incident-analysis.md](../../templates/ihl-incident-analysis.md) for where it goes in your analysis.

### How you know it worked

Your brief quotes the key paragraph exactly with its number, states the holding in one sentence that a lawyer from either side would accept as accurate, and lists what the ruling does not decide.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing *Beit Sourik* as "the Court ruled the barrier illegal" | Wrong; it upheld the purpose and cancelled specific segments | Quote the holding for the segment concerned | Read the outcome section, not only the headnote |
| Asking the Court to declare the occupation or settlements unlawful as such | The Court does not decide petitions on that basis | Ask about a specific order, route or decision | Frame the remedy around one decision |
| Stretching a ruling beyond its facts | The State distinguishes it in one paragraph | Use the closest holding; say where it stops | Write "what it does not decide" for every ruling |
| Relying on a news report of a Hebrew-only judgment | Errors in case number, date or holding | Find the judgment on the court website, or say you rely on a secondary account | Record the source you actually read |
| Presenting only critiques from your side | Reads as advocacy | Add an attributed critique from the other direction | Use the "critiques" list |

## Review

- In the territories the Supreme Court applies the Hague Regulations as custom, GC IV's humanitarian provisions on the basis of the State's declaration, and Israeli administrative law, including proportionality.
- It weighs ICJ opinions but decides on its own facts (*Mara'abe*, para. 74).
- Leading holdings: requisition needs a real military need (*Elon Moreh*); no physical interrogation means without legislation (1999); assigned residence only for a person who is personally dangerous (*Ajuri*); route-by-route proportionality (*Beit Sourik*, *Mara'abe*); case-by-case review of targeted killings (2006); no occupation of Gaza but duties for essential humanitarian needs (*Al-Bassiouni*, *Gisha*).
- Critics say the Court does too little (human-rights scholars) and too much (Kohelet, the *Silwad* dissent); report both.
- Use each ruling for what it held, from whichever side you are on.
