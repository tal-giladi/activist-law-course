# 15.2 · The Law of Occupation

The law of occupation governs a state that controls territory outside its sovereign borders through its army: what it must do for the population, what it may not do, and when the occupation begins and ends. In this lesson you learn the test for occupation, the occupant's main duties, and the competing positions on the West Bank (Judea and Samaria), East Jerusalem and Gaza, and you finish by building a position map that puts each position next to its source.

> [!NOTE]
> Whether, and how, the law of occupation applies to these territories is one of the most contested questions in international law. The course takes no position. Each view below is attributed to whoever holds it, and the quiz tests the rules and who says what, never which view is right.

## Why it matters

Almost every international-law argument about the territories starts with occupation: petitions about land and roads, debates about settlements, Gaza crossings and humanitarian aid, and advisory opinions of the ICJ. Settlers' councils invoke the commander's duty to keep order and safety; Palestinian villages invoke protection of private property; the State relies on security needs and on its own legal positions; UN bodies apply the Fourth Geneva Convention in full. If you cannot state the test and the duties, and who disagrees about what, you cannot follow, let alone join, any of these arguments.

## The law

### When territory is occupied

> Art. 42. Territory is considered occupied when it is actually placed under the authority of the hostile army.
>
> The occupation extends only to the territory where such authority has been established and can be exercised.

*Gist:* occupation is a question of fact (effective control), and it covers only the area where that control exists (Hague Regulations, art. 42).

### The occupant's general duty

> Art. 43. The authority of the legitimate power having in fact passed into the hands of the occupant, the latter shall take all the measures in his power to restore, and ensure, as far as possible, public order and safety, while respecting, unless absolutely prevented, the laws in force in the country.

*Gist:* the occupant governs temporarily: it must keep public order and civil life and keep the existing law unless absolutely prevented (art. 43). GC IV art. 64 similarly keeps local penal law in force, subject to security needs.

### Property

> Art. 46. Family honour and rights, the lives of persons, and private property, as well as religious convictions and practice, must be respected.
>
> Private property cannot be confiscated.

> Art. 52. Requisitions in kind and services shall not be demanded from municipalities or inhabitants except for the needs of the army of occupation.

> Art. 55. The occupying State shall be regarded only as administrator and usufructuary of public buildings, real estate, forests, and agricultural estates belonging to the hostile State, and situated in the occupied country.

*Gist:* private property is protected; requisition is allowed only for the army's needs; public property is administered, not owned (Hague Regulations arts 46, 52, 55). GC IV art. 53 bans destruction of property "except where such destruction is rendered absolutely necessary by military operations".

Full text: [Hague Regulations (Avalon)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/hague04.asp).

### The population (Fourth Geneva Convention)

> Art. 47. Protected persons who are in occupied territory shall not be deprived, in any case or in any manner whatsoever, of the benefits of the present Convention by any change introduced, as the result of the occupation of a territory, into the institutions or government of the said territory, nor by any agreement concluded between the authorities of the occupied territories and the Occupying Power, nor by any annexation by the latter of the whole or part of the occupied territory.

> Art. 49. Individual or mass forcible transfers, as well as deportations of protected persons from occupied territory to the territory of the Occupying Power or to that of any other country, occupied or not, are prohibited, regardless of their motive.
>
> […]
>
> The Occupying Power shall not deport or transfer parts of its own civilian population into the territory it occupies.

> Art. 55. To the fullest extent of the means available to it, the Occupying Power has the duty of ensuring the food and medical supplies of the population; […]

> Art. 59. If the whole or part of the population of an occupied territory is inadequately supplied, the Occupying Power shall agree to relief schemes on behalf of the said population, and shall facilitate them by all the means at its disposal.

*Gist:* the population keeps its protection whatever agreements or annexation occur (art. 47); forcible transfers and deportations are banned, and so is the occupant's transfer of its own population (art. 49, first and sixth paragraphs); the occupant must ensure supplies and facilitate relief (arts 55, 59). Under art. 6, most of the Convention ceases to apply one year after the general close of military operations, but listed articles, including 47, 49, 53 and 59, continue for as long as the occupant exercises the functions of government.

Full text: [GC IV (Avalon)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva07.asp).

## How it works in practice

### The test, step by step

1. **Is a foreign army present without the consent of the territory's government?**
2. **Can it exercise authority there** (the capacity to impose its orders), and does it?
3. **Has the local government been displaced** from exercising authority in that area?

The [ICRC](https://www.icrc.org/en/law-and-policy/occupation) describes occupation as "an unconsented-to effective control over a territory to which it has no sovereign title" and notes that it can be "difficult … to determine when an occupation begins and ends". Two approaches compete for the edges:

- **The classic (binary) approach:** a territory is occupied or it is not. When the occupant withdraws its ground forces and the local authority governs, occupation ends.
- **The functional approach:** where a former occupant keeps control over some functions (borders, airspace, registries), the duties linked to those functions continue. The ICJ applied a version of this to Gaza in 2024 and 2025; critics question its legal basis (see below).

### The three areas: who says what

#### Judea and Samaria (the West Bank)

| Who | Position |
|---|---|
| **Israel's government** | The territory "is best regarded as territory over which there are competing claims which should be resolved in peace process negotiations". It questions "whether the Fourth Geneva Convention applies de jure to territory such as the West Bank over which there was no previous legitimate sovereign", and argues that art. 49(6) does not cover voluntary settlement: "the case of Jews voluntarily establishing homes and communities in their ancestral homeland … does not match the kind of forced population transfers contemplated by Article 49(6)" ([MFA, archived 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/20160102015819/http://www.mfa.gov.il/mfa/foreignpolicy/peace/guide/pages/israeli%20settlements%20and%20international%20law.aspx)). As the Supreme Court records, "The State of Israel has declared that it practices the humanitarian parts of this convention" (*Mara'abe*, para. 14). |
| **Israel's Supreme Court** | "The Judea and Samaria areas are held by the State of Israel in belligerent occupation" (*Mara'abe*, 2005, para. 14). It applies the Hague Regulations as custom and the humanitarian provisions of GC IV on the basis of the State's declaration, without deciding the Convention's formal applicability (lesson 15.3). |
| **The ICJ** | GC IV "is applicable in the Palestinian territories which before the conflict lay to the east of the Green Line" (2004, para. 101; repeated in 2024, para. 96). Art. 49(6) prohibits "any measures taken by an occupying Power in order to organize or encourage transfers" (2004, para. 120); the transfer of settlers "is contrary to the sixth paragraph of Article 49" (2024, para. 119). |
| **UN Security Council** | Settlements have "no legal validity and constitute a flagrant violation under international law" (resolution 2334 (2016), para. 1). |

#### East Jerusalem

Israel applied its law to East Jerusalem in 1967, and Basic Law: Jerusalem, the Capital of Israel provides:

> ירושלים השלמה והמאוחדת היא בירת ישראל.

*Gist:* "Jerusalem, complete and united, is the capital of Israel" (Basic Law: Jerusalem, s. 1; [Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_ירושלים_בירת_ישראל)). In Israeli law East Jerusalem is part of Israel. The ICJ (2004, 2024) and the Security Council treat it as occupied territory, citing GC IV art. 47 and the bar on acquiring territory by force.

#### Gaza after the 2005 disengagement

| Who | Position |
|---|---|
| **Israel's government** | "As Israel does not exercise effective control over the Gaza Strip, the legal framework governing the present hostilities is not the law of belligerent occupation, but rather the rules of LOAC, in particular those governing the conduct of hostilities" (MFA, November 2023). |
| **Israel's Supreme Court** | Since September 2005 "Israel no longer has effective control over what happens in the Gaza Strip"; its main obligations "derive from the state of armed conflict", from "the degree of control exercised by the State of Israel over the border crossings", and from "the relationship that was created … after the years of Israeli military rule" (*Al-Bassiouni*, 2008, para. 12). Commentators often call this a *sui generis* position: no occupation, but some residual duties. In March 2025 (HCJ 2280/24, *Gisha*) the Court again found no belligerent occupation during the current war (lesson 15.3). |
| **The ICJ** | Israel "remained capable of exercising, and continued to exercise, certain key elements of authority over the Gaza Strip", so its withdrawal "has not entirely released it of its obligations under the law of occupation", which are "commensurate with the degree of its effective control" (2024, paras 93–94). In 2025 it found that since 7 October 2023 Israel's control, and so its obligations, "increased significantly" (paras 85–87). |
| **Scholars and NGOs** | Pnina Sharvit-Baruch, writing for the Jerusalem Center for Public Affairs, argues that Israel does not have effective control under the established test (JCPA, c. 2011). Gisha, an Israeli NGO, argues the functional view: "the international law of occupation continues to apply to Israel in the spheres in which it continues to exercise control over the lives of Palestinian residents" (Bashi and Feldman, *Scale of Control*, 7.11.2011). Sean Watts (Lieber Institute, 2025) accepts that parts of Gaza may be occupied but warns that the Court's "à la carte" functional approach has an unclear legal basis. |

### How to write about it

- Say "the ICJ found …", "Israel's government says …", "the Supreme Court held …". Never "the West Bank is occupied" or "the territory is disputed" in your own voice, unless you are writing your own advocacy and say so.
- Use each actor's terms in quotation marks when you report their view: "Judea and Samaria", "the Occupied Palestinian Territory", "disputed territories".
- Quote the article you rely on and say whose interpretation you follow.

## Worked example

Two petitions are drafted in the same week, and a volunteer at a legal-information desk checks both for accuracy.

**Petition 1 — a village council.** Farmers say a military order requisitions their private land for a security road. The draft says: "The occupation is illegal, so every order is void." The volunteer marks it: the "illegal occupation" claim is the ICJ's 2024 opinion (an advisory opinion, contested by Israel and by four judges, lesson 15.4), and the Supreme Court does not decide petitions on that basis. The workable argument is art. 52 (requisition only for the army's needs) and art. 46 (private property), which the Court applies as custom, plus proportionality (lesson 15.3).

**Petition 2 — a regional council of Israeli communities.** Residents ask the commander to fence and light a road after repeated stone-throwing. The draft says: "International law does not apply in Judea and Samaria, so the commander must act under Israeli law." The volunteer marks it too: the Supreme Court holds that the area is held in belligerent occupation and that the commander's power and duty come from art. 43, which covers the safety of everyone in the area, including Israeli residents (*Mara'abe*, paras 14, 18–19; lesson 13.5). Arguing that the law of occupation does not apply would undercut the very duty the council relies on.

**What both show.** Each side was tempted to state its political conclusion as law. Both petitions get stronger when they use the rule the court actually applies.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The Hague Regulations and GC IV texts linked above, and the sources in this lesson.
- About 100 minutes: 50 minutes for the assigned lecture, 50 minutes to build your position map.

### Steps

1. Copy into a table the nine provisions in "The law" (Hague 42, 43, 46, 52, 55; GC IV 47, 49, 55/59, 53), one line each: what it requires and what facts would show a breach.
2. Make a second table with three rows (West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza) and five columns (Israel's government, Israel's Supreme Court, ICJ, UN Security Council or other UN body, one scholar). Fill each cell with a short quote and its source and date. Leave a cell empty, marked "not found", rather than guess.
3. Add one row for the occupation test applied to a fictional case: "State A's army withdrew its ground troops from area B two years ago but controls B's airspace, sea and the only commercial crossing; a local armed group governs inside." Write how the binary and the functional approach would each answer, without choosing.
4. Underline any sentence in your tables that is written in your own voice and rewrite it as an attributed sentence.

### Template

Put the position map in section 2 ("Applicable framework") of the [IHL incident analysis template](../../templates/ihl-incident-analysis.md).

### How you know it worked

Every cell has a quote, a source and a date, or says "not found". A reader cannot tell from your tables which view you hold.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing "the territory is occupied" or "the territory is disputed" as fact | Your text takes a side and loses readers on the other | Attribute: "the ICJ found", "Israel's government says" | Use the position map |
| Treating occupation as a matter of labels or intentions | Misses the legal test, which is factual control | Apply art. 42 to the facts | Start every analysis with the three-step test |
| Assuming no occupation means no obligations | Ignores conduct-of-hostilities rules and the duties the Supreme Court found in *Al-Bassiouni* | List the duties that apply on each view | Write both columns: occupation and no occupation |
| Quoting art. 49(6) without the competing readings | One-sided; the State's answer is predictable | Give the ICJ's reading and Israel's reading, each attributed | Quote both sources |
| Arguing in an Israeli court that the occupation is unlawful as such | The Court does not decide petitions on that basis | Use the rules the Court applies: arts 43, 46, 52, proportionality | Read lesson 15.3 first |

## Review

- Territory is occupied where a hostile army actually exercises authority over it (Hague art. 42); the test is factual.
- The occupant must keep order and civil life (art. 43), respect private property (arts 46, 52), act only as administrator of public property (art. 55), not transfer populations (GC IV art. 49) and ensure supplies and relief (arts 55, 59).
- On the West Bank, Israel's government argues the territory is disputed and that GC IV's de jure application is questionable, while applying its humanitarian provisions; the Supreme Court treats it as held in belligerent occupation; the ICJ and the Security Council apply GC IV in full.
- On Gaza, Israel's government and Supreme Court say there has been no occupation since 2005, with some duties remaining; the ICJ found obligations commensurate with Israel's control.
- Report each view with its source; keep your own conclusion out unless you clearly label it.
