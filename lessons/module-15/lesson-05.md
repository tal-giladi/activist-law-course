# 15.5 · Analysing an Incident Under IHL, Neutrally

When a strike, a raid or a rocket attack makes the news, people want a verdict within hours. A careful analyst gives something more useful: what is known, which rules apply, what each rule needs, what is missing, and how the parties read the rules differently. In this lesson you learn a six-step method and apply it to a detailed hypothetical incident, and you finish with a two-page analysis written with the IHL incident analysis template.

> [!NOTE]
> This lesson uses an invented incident, on purpose. The course does not analyse real named incidents or reach conclusions about them, and it takes no position on the conflict. The method works the same for an attack by any party. Collecting evidence in conflict zones is out of scope: the method is for desk work with public sources. Module 17 covers documentation.

## Why it matters

Fast verdicts fail in both directions: some are later contradicted by facts, and some dismiss real violations because the first claim was overstated. Courts, investigators, journalists and diplomats take seriously the analyst who shows the work: sources graded, rules quoted, gaps admitted, and the other side's reading stated fairly. This lesson gives you that method, and it is the core of the module project.

## The law

The rules are the ones from lesson 15.1. For an attack, quote them in this order:

> Rule 1. The parties to the conflict must at all times distinguish between civilians and combatants. Attacks may only be directed against combatants. Attacks must not be directed against civilians.

> Rule 14. Launching an attack which may be expected to cause incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians, damage to civilian objects, or a combination thereof, which would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated, is prohibited.

> Rule 15. In the conduct of military operations, constant care must be taken to spare the civilian population, civilians and civilian objects. All feasible precautions must be taken to avoid, and in any event to minimize, incidental loss of civilian life, injury to civilians and damage to civilian objects.

> Rule 20. Each party to the conflict must give effective advance warning of attacks which may affect the civilian population, unless circumstances do not permit.

> Rule 22. The parties to the conflict must take all feasible precautions to protect the civilian population and civilian objects under their control against the effects of attacks.

> Rule 97. The use of human shields is prohibited.

*Gist:* distinction, proportionality and precautions bind the attacker; precautions against the effects of attacks and the ban on human shields bind the defender. All are marked as applying in international and non-international conflicts. Source: [ICRC list of customary rules (2005)](https://www.icrc.org/sites/default/files/external/doc/en/assets/files/other/icrc_002_0860.pdf); Rule 8 (military objectives) and Rule 6 (direct participation) are in lesson 15.1.

## How it works in practice

### The six steps

1. **Facts.** Build a timeline. For every fact, record the source, who produced it, how (sensor, witness, document), when, and whether anyone disputes it. Mark each fact *established*, *claimed by X* or *unknown*.
2. **Framework.** Is there an armed conflict? Which classification does each actor use? Is the area occupied on anyone's view? Which instruments bind each party? Write the competing positions, not your own (lesson 15.2).
3. **Rules.** Apply distinction, proportionality and precautions to the attacker, and precautions and the human-shields rule to the defender. For each, write what facts the rule needs.
4. **Evidence gaps.** List what you would need to know and do not: the attacker's intelligence at the time, what advantage it expected, whether a warning was given and understood, whether the defender used the site.
5. **Competing interpretations.** Where the parties read a rule differently, state each reading with its source.
6. **Limited conclusion.** Say what the evidence supports, what it does not, and what would change the conclusion. Never more than the evidence allows.

### Grading sources

| Source | Strength | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Official statement of the attacking party | Knows its intelligence and intent | Interested party; often releases little evidence at first |
| Statement of the defending party or authority it controls | Knows the site and casualties | Interested party; may not separate civilians from fighters |
| Independent investigations (UN commissions, ICRC public statements, national inquiries) | Methodology and mandate on record | Access limits; each side disputes mandates it considers biased (lesson 14.5) |
| NGO reports | Witness interviews, field research | Varying methods; check how they verified |
| Open-source material (video, satellite images) | Can fix time and place | Can be old, edited or mislocated; needs verification (Module 17) |
| News reports | Fast | Often second-hand; early numbers change |

### Two interpretations you will meet often

**What does "excessive" mean, and judged from when?**

- Israel's government: "a proportionality assessment is based on the military commander's judgement at the time of the attack, not on hindsight: the test is conduct-oriented, not result-oriented" (MFA, November 2023); "the relevant analysis is forward-looking, based on what a reasonable military commander could anticipate" (2015 report, para. 332). The Supreme Court in *Targeted Killings* (para. 46) called the balancing "difficult" and required case-by-case examination.
- The UN Commission of Inquiry on the 2014 conflict reasoned from patterns: "the weapons used, the timing of attacks, and the fact that the targets were located in densely populated areas indicate that the Israel Defense Forces may not have done everything feasible to avoid or limit civilian casualties" (A/HRC/29/52, para. 41).
- *Neutral reading:* both accept that the legal test is prospective. They differ on what evidence may show what was expected, and on how much weight outcomes and patterns carry.

**What makes a warning "effective"?**

- Israel's 2015 report describes "roof knocking" (a low-explosive strike on a roof before the main strike) as "a progressive precaution that went beyond the requirements of international law" and says assessments show it was "highly effective" (para. 313).
- The 2015 Commission concluded that "roof knocks" "cannot be considered an effective warning given the confusion they often cause to building residents and the short time allowed to evacuate" (para. 42).
- *Neutral reading:* the dispute is about facts (did people understand, was there time?) and about the standard (what "effective" requires). An analysis of any warning should record how it was given, when, to whom, and whether it was understood.

### Who investigates

The attacking state's own system is usually the first formal step. Israel's MFA describes the IDF's mechanisms: the Military Advocate General, a Fact-Finding and Assessment Mechanism and criminal investigations ([MFA IHL page](https://israelihl.mfa.gov.il/ihl)). Human-rights groups, UN commissions (lesson 14.5) and international courts (Module 16) may also examine incidents, and each side disputes some of these mechanisms. Note in your analysis which bodies have examined the incident and what they found, each attributed.

## Worked example

### The incident (invented)

State Norland is in an armed conflict with the Valley Front, an organised armed group that governs the town of Kessar across the border. At 14:10 on a weekday, a Norland aircraft strikes a three-storey building in central Kessar.

- **Norland's army** says, the same evening, that the building housed a Valley Front command room on the ground floor, that rockets were fired from 200 metres away that morning, that an officer phoned a resident 20 minutes before the strike, and that the attack was approved after a collateral-damage assessment. It releases a 30-second video of the strike and no other material.
- **Kessar's health office**, run by the Valley Front administration, says nine people died, including four children, and does not say whether any were fighters.
- **A local NGO** interviews three residents: two say there was no warning; one says a neighbour got a call "a few minutes before".
- **Satellite images** published two days later show the building destroyed and the neighbouring building damaged.

### The analysis

**1. Facts.** *Established:* a strike on that building at about 14:10 (video and satellite images agree). *Claimed by Norland:* a command room; rocket fire nearby; a warning call 20 minutes before. *Claimed by the health office:* nine dead, including four children. *Disputed:* whether a warning reached the residents and how long before. *Unknown:* who was in the building, and what Norland expected.

**2. Framework.** An armed conflict between a state and an organised armed group: Norland and others may classify it differently; distinction, proportionality and precautions apply either way. Neither side claims the area is occupied.

**3. Rules.**
- *Distinction (Rules 1, 8):* was the building, or part of it, a military objective at 14:10? It depends on whether the command room existed and was in use.
- *Proportionality (Rule 14):* what civilian harm did Norland expect, and what concrete and direct military advantage did it anticipate? The outcome (nine dead) is relevant evidence but not the test itself.
- *Precautions (Rules 15–20):* did Norland verify the target, choose the time and weapon to reduce harm, and give an *effective* warning?
- *The Valley Front (Rules 22, 97):* if it placed a command room in a residential building, did it take feasible precautions to protect residents, and were residents used as shields? These duties do not cancel Norland's.

**4. Evidence gaps.** Norland's intelligence and collateral-damage estimate; the identity and status of the nine dead; phone records of the warning; whether the ground floor was in military use (independent evidence); the time of day and school hours.

**5. Competing interpretations.** Norland is likely to say that the commander acted reasonably on the information at the time. Critics are likely to say that a strike on a residential building in daytime, with children killed, suggests that precautions were inadequate. Both readings are in the "two interpretations" section above; quote them, attributed.

**6. Limited conclusion.** "On the public evidence, the strike's lawfulness cannot be determined. If the command room was in use and Norland reasonably expected few civilians to be present after an effective warning, the attack may have complied with IHL. If the building had no military use, or Norland expected heavy civilian harm for a small advantage, or the warning was not effective, it may not have. The evidence that would decide it is listed in step 4. Norland's and the Valley Front's own duties are both engaged."

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The [IHL incident analysis template](../../templates/ihl-incident-analysis.md).
- Your rules card (15.1), position map (15.2), ruling brief (15.3) and opinion matrix (15.4).
- About 120 minutes: 60 minutes for the assigned lecture, 60 minutes to write.

### Steps

1. Change three facts of the Kessar incident to build your own variation: for example, the strike is at 03:00; Norland releases intercepted communications; an independent NGO confirms that the ground floor stored weapons; or the defending group fired from a school. Keep it invented.
2. Fill in sections 1–6 of the template for your variation.
3. In section 6, quote at least two competing interpretations from this module, each with its source.
4. Write the limited conclusion in no more than 150 words. Include one sentence on what would change it.
5. Swap sides: reread your analysis as Norland's legal adviser, then as the Valley Front's spokesperson, and as a human-rights researcher. Mark every sentence each of them would call unfair; fix those that are unfair, not those that are only unwelcome.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not travel to a conflict area, contact armed groups, or collect evidence on the ground for this exercise. Analysing real incidents can expose you to legal and personal risk and can harm witnesses. Use invented facts here; Module 17 covers safe documentation.

### How you know it worked

Every fact has a source and a status; every rule is quoted; the gaps list says what evidence would decide each rule; both parties' duties are addressed; the conclusion does not go beyond the evidence; and readers from opposite sides can each find their best argument fairly stated.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting from the conclusion | The analysis reads as advocacy and is dismissed | Rebuild from step 1 | Write the facts table before any rule |
| Treating one party's statement as established fact | Overclaims; collapses when contradicted | Mark it "claimed by X" | Use the source-grading table |
| Judging proportionality only from casualties | Misstates the legal test | Ask what was expected and anticipated | Write the evidence gaps for Rule 14 |
| Ignoring the defender's duties | One-sided; misses Rules 22 and 97 | Apply the rules to both parties | Use the both-parties checklist |
| Analysing a real named incident with a verdict | Risk of error, defamation and harm to people involved; outside this course | Use an invented scenario, or describe the debate without a verdict | Keep your practice invented |

## Review

- Six steps: facts, framework, rules, evidence gaps, competing interpretations, limited conclusion.
- Grade every source and keep "claimed by" separate from "established".
- Apply distinction, proportionality and precautions to the attacker, and precautions and the human-shields ban to the defender.
- The proportionality test is prospective; the parties differ on what evidence shows what was expected.
- A good conclusion states what the evidence can and cannot support, and what would change it.
