# 13.5 · International Law Inside Israeli Courts

Israeli courts do apply international law, but by two different routes: customary rules enter Israeli law automatically, while treaties enter only through Knesset legislation, and Israeli statutes are read, where possible, in line with Israel's international obligations. In this lesson you learn the leading rulings, the presumption of compatibility and the debate over it, and you finish by writing an international-law section for an Israeli letter or petition.

## Why it matters

Petitions to the High Court regularly cite international law: landowners and villages in Judea and Samaria (the West Bank; the term used in Israeli legislation) cite the Hague Regulations; residents and settlers' councils cite the occupying power's duty to keep public order and safety; asylum seekers' organisations cite the Refugee Convention; disability and children's groups cite the CRPD and the CRC; and the State cites international law to defend its decisions. Whether the argument succeeds often depends on one question the court asks first: *is this rule customary, or is it a treaty the Knesset never legislated?* If you build your section on the right route, the court engages with it. If you cite a treaty as if it were a statute, the State answers in one paragraph and the court moves on.

## The law

### Custom is part of Israeli law; treaties are not, unless legislated

> כפי שכבר אמרנו בפרשת בית-אל, מבחינים בין משפט בינלאומי מנהגי לבין משפט בינלאומי הסכמי. הראשון הוא חלק מהמשפט המוניציפאלי, ואילו השני אינו חלק ממנו, אלא אם כן נתקבל בדרך חקיקה לאומית.

*Gist:* customary international law is part of Israeli law; treaty law is not, unless adopted by national legislation (Justice Witkon).

> את עיקר הדיון אוכל לקיים עתה במשולב לגבי שתי הבחינות הללו גם יחד, כי הרי המשפט הבינלאומי המנהגי מהווה ממילא חלק מן הדין הישראלי

*Gist:* the court can examine Israeli law and customary international law together, because custom is in any case part of Israeli law (Acting President Landau).

Source: [בג״ץ 390/79 דויקאת נ׳ ממשלת ישראל (אלון מורה), 1979](https://hamoked.org.il/files/2016/1670.pdf) (Hebrew judgment as published by HaMoked); English: [Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/dweikat-et-al-v-state). In the same case the Court explained that it examined the requisition under the Hague Regulations (custom) and refrained from ruling on Art. 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention, which it treated as treaty law not enforceable in an Israeli court.

### Custom gives way to a contrary statute

*Abu Aita* (HCJ 69/81, 1983), in Versa's English headnote: "Customary international law is automatically incorporated into Israeli law, and becomes part of it", "except when it is in direct conflict with enacted Israeli law, in which case, Israeli law takes precedence"; conventional law becomes part of Israeli law "only if it is adopted or combined with Israeli law by enactment of primary or subsidiary legislation". The free Hebrew original was not found online for this lesson; the citation is פ״ד לז(2) 197. Source: [Versa — Abu Aita](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/aita-v-regional-commander-judea-and-samaria).

*Targeted Killings* (HCJ 769/02, 2006), para. 19 (Versa English): customary laws "are a part of Israeli law", while international law enshrined in conventions that do not reflect custom "is not a part of the internal law of the State of Israel". Source: [Versa — Targeted Killings](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/public-committee-against-torture-v-government).

### The presumption of compatibility, and its open edge

> "חזקת ההתאמה הפרשנית" היא כלל פרשני שאומץ בפסיקה, הקובע שיש לפרש ככל שניתן את דיני המדינה באופן התואם את המשפט הבינלאומי, במטרה לצמצם את החיכוך שבין המשפט המדינתי והבינלאומי.

*Gist:* the presumption of compatibility is a rule of interpretation adopted by the courts: Israeli law is read, as far as possible, in line with international law (Bohrer, describing the case law, citing among others HCJ 2599/00 *Yated* (2002)).

> השאלה אם חזקת ההתאמה הפרשנית חלה גם על חוקי־היסוד טרם הוכרעה בדין הישראלי

*Gist:* whether the presumption also applies to Basic Laws has not been decided (HCJ 5555/18 *Hasson v. Knesset*, 8.7.2021, as quoted by Bohrer).

Source: [זיו בורר, עיוני משפט מו 175 (2022)](https://www.taulawreview.sites.tau.ac.il/_files/ugd/6a76e2_469150003ab945cab43278c021cb754f.pdf), abstract and notes 73–74. The Hebrew judgment in *Hasson* is on the Supreme Court's website (not opened for this lesson ⚠️).

## How it works in practice

### Two routes, one table

| Your international source | Route into an Israeli court | What you ask the court to do |
|---|---|---|
| A customary rule (e.g. the Hague Regulations on property in occupied territory) | Automatic, unless a Knesset statute clearly contradicts it | Apply the rule directly, alongside Israeli administrative law |
| A treaty the Knesset legislated (e.g. the Hague Child Abduction Convention Law) | Through the statute | Apply the statute |
| A treaty Israel ratified but did not legislate (e.g. the CRC, the CRPD, the ICCPR) | Presumption of compatibility | Interpret the Israeli law or exercise of discretion in line with the treaty |
| A treaty Israel did not ratify, a General Assembly resolution, a committee's view | Persuasive only, if at all | Use as background; never as your main ground |

### The debate, in both voices

- **For a broad presumption.** Bohrer (2022) argues that the presumption applies to all Israeli norms and should apply to Basic Laws too, as a way to reduce friction between Israeli and international law.
- **For a narrow presumption.** The Kohelet Policy Forum (Shalev, 2025) argues that Israel's system is dualist, that in theory the presumption covers only custom and treaties Israel joined, notes that the United States and the United Kingdom use it only for ambiguous statutes, and criticises rulings such as *Yated* for using it too widely. According to Bohrer, the Government and the Knesset also argued, in *Silwad* (HCJ 1308/17, 2020) and other cases, against applying the presumption at the constitutional level.
- **Israel's official positions** appear in the State's responses to petitions and on the Foreign Ministry's [legal site](https://israelihl.mfa.gov.il/general_international_law). In the territories, the State has argued that the Fourth Geneva Convention as a whole is not customary while declaring that it applies its humanitarian provisions; the Court in *Ajuri* (2002) ruled on that basis without deciding the question.

You do not need to win this debate to use international law well. Argue within what is settled: custom applies; legislated treaties apply; ratified treaties guide interpretation of ambiguous law and of discretion.

### How to write the international-law section of a petition or letter

1. **Lead with Israeli law.** Your main ground is a statute, a Basic Law or an administrative-law principle (Modules 3–5). International law supports it.
2. **Name the route.** "This rule is part of customary international law, which forms part of Israeli law (בג״ץ 390/79; בג״ץ 69/81)" or "Israel ratified the Convention on [date]; under the presumption of compatibility, section X of the Law should be read in line with Article Y".
3. **Quote the text exactly.** The article in English, with an official Hebrew translation if one exists or your own marked "תרגום חופשי".
4. **Prove custom if you claim it.** Two or three items of state practice and opinio juris (lesson 13.3), or a ruling that already recognised the rule.
5. **Check reservations and status** (lesson 13.2) and say so: "Israel did not enter a reservation to this article."
6. **State Israel's position and answer it.** If the State has taken a position on this rule, quote it and explain why it does not change the result, or why your argument fits within it.
7. **Keep the remedy Israeli.** You ask the court to cancel a decision, read a section a certain way, or order an authority to act; not to "enforce the Convention".

## Worked example

Two petitions, from different parts of the spectrum, show the same method.

### Petition A: a village council and a landowner

A Palestinian landowner and the village council challenge a military order requisitioning private land near a village in Judea and Samaria "for military needs".

1. **Israeli ground:** administrative law: authority, relevant considerations, proportionality (Module 4); the commander's power derives from the law of belligerent occupation.
2. **Custom:** Articles 46 and 52 of the Hague Regulations protect private property and limit requisition to the needs of the army. They are customary and therefore part of Israeli law (*Elon Moreh*; *Abu Aita*).
3. **Treaty:** they mention GC IV only briefly, noting the State's position on its application and that the Court has applied its humanitarian provisions on the basis of the State's declaration (*Ajuri*).
4. **Remedy:** an order nisi to show cause why the requisition order should not be cancelled.

### Petition B: a settlers' local council

A local council of Israeli communities in Judea and Samaria petitions against the military commander's failure to repair and secure a road on which residents have been attacked.

1. **Israeli ground:** the commander's duty to exercise his discretion reasonably; failure to act (lesson 04.3).
2. **Custom:** Article 43 of the Hague Regulations obliges the occupying authority to take all measures in its power to restore and ensure public order and safety, and the Supreme Court has held that this authority covers any person in the area, not only "protected persons", and extends to protecting Israelis living there (*Mara'abe*, HCJ 7957/04, 2005, paras 18–19; Module 15).
3. **Treaty:** none needed; the customary duty suffices.
4. **Remedy:** an order nisi to show cause why the commander should not adopt a security plan for the road within a set time.

**What both show.** Each petition leads with Israeli administrative law, uses a customary rule the Court already accepts, treats the treaty argument with care, and asks for an Israeli remedy. The international law is the same; only the facts and the side differ.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your source table (13.1), treaty check (13.2), custom check (13.3) and forum notes (13.4).
- A real or realistic decision you would challenge, or a letter you would send, from your own cause.
- 40 minutes for the assigned lecture, 40 minutes for the two Hebrew readings and 50 minutes for writing.

### Steps

1. Write the Israeli legal ground in two sentences (the statute, Basic Law or administrative-law principle).
2. Choose up to two international sources from your earlier tables that support it.
3. For each, write the route: custom (with the rulings that it is part of Israeli law), legislated treaty (with the statute), or ratified treaty (with the presumption of compatibility).
4. Quote each provision exactly, with the ratification date and reservation check for treaties, and two pieces of evidence for custom.
5. Find Israel's position on the point (a State response, an MFA statement, a court summary of the State's argument) and write one paragraph answering it.
6. Draft the international-law section in Hebrew using the template, no longer than one page.
7. Re-read it as the State Attorney: underline every sentence they could attack, and fix it.

> This is a dry run. If you ever file it: act in your own name only. This is not legal advice — check current rules before filing.

### How you know it worked

Your section opens with Israeli law, names the route for each international source, quotes it exactly, includes Israel's position with a source, and asks for an Israeli remedy. A reader can tell in ten seconds which rules are custom, which are treaty, and why the court can use each.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asking the court to "enforce" a treaty Israel never legislated | The State replies that the treaty is not part of Israeli law; the argument fails | Recast it as interpretation of an Israeli law under the presumption | Name the route for every source |
| Claiming a rule is customary without evidence or a ruling | The State disputes it and the court does not decide the point | Add practice and opinio juris, or a ruling recognising the rule | Run the custom check from lesson 13.3 |
| Leading with international law instead of Israeli law | The court sees no Israeli ground and may dismiss | Restructure: Israeli ground first, international law in support | Use the template's order |
| Ignoring a contrary statute | Custom gives way to a clear Knesset statute (*Abu Aita*) | Argue the statute can be read compatibly, or drop the point | Search for a contrary statute before writing |
| Omitting the State's known position | Looks one-sided; the court hears it first from the State | Quote it and answer it | Check MFA sources and earlier State responses |
| Quoting UN committee views as binding | The State answers that they are not binding | Present them as persuasive and attributed | Separate the treaty text from its interpreters |

## Review

- Customary international law is part of Israeli law unless a Knesset statute contradicts it (*Elon Moreh*, *Abu Aita*, *Targeted Killings*).
- Treaties bind Israel internationally but apply in court only through legislation; otherwise they guide interpretation.
- The presumption of compatibility reads Israeli law, as far as possible, in line with Israel's obligations; whether it reaches Basic Laws is undecided and debated.
- An international-law section leads with Israeli law, names each source's route, quotes exactly, states Israel's position and asks for an Israeli remedy.
- The method is the same whichever side of a dispute you are on.
