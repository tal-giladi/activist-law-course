# 13.1 · What International Law Is

International law is the law between states: there is no world parliament and no world police, yet states sign treaties, follow customs, and argue in courts in the language of law every day. In this lesson you learn where international law comes from, who it binds, and how to tell a binding rule from a persuasive opinion, and you finish by building a source table for your own cause.

## Why it matters

Activists across the spectrum quote international law: a human-rights group cites a UN committee, a settlers' council cites the Hague Regulations, a religious-freedom group cites the ICCPR, a pro-Israel group cites the UN Charter's right of self-defence. Many of these arguments fail before a court or an official for one reason: the speaker mixed up what binds Israel with what only persuades. A General Assembly resolution, a Special Rapporteur's report and a treaty Israel ratified carry very different weight. If you sort your sources correctly, your letter or petition is taken seriously; if not, the other side dismisses the whole argument by pointing at the weakest source.

## The law

### The sources the World Court applies

> 1. The Court, whose function is to decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it, shall apply:
> a. international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the contesting states;
> b. international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law;
> c. the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations;
> d. subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.

*Gist:* treaties, custom and general principles are the sources of international law; court decisions and the best scholarship help you find the rules but do not create them.

Source: [Statute of the ICJ, Art. 38(1)](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute). The phrase "civilized nations" is a 1945 wording that today is read as "the community of nations".

### Court decisions bind only the parties

> The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case.

*Gist:* there is no formal rule of precedent in international law; even a judgment of the World Court binds only the states in that case.

Source: [Statute of the ICJ, Art. 59](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute).

### Who may sue in the World Court

> Only states may be parties in cases before the Court.

*Gist:* individuals, NGOs and companies cannot bring or join a contentious case at the ICJ (lesson 13.4).

Source: [Statute of the ICJ, Art. 34(1)](https://www.icj-cij.org/statute).

### How custom is proven

> To determine the existence and content of a rule of customary international law, it is necessary to ascertain whether there is a general practice that is accepted as law (opinio juris).

*Gist:* a customary rule needs two things: what states actually do, and their sense that they are legally bound to do it (lesson 13.3).

Source: [ILC Conclusions on identification of customary international law (2018), conclusion 2](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf).

## How it works in practice

### Three differences from Israeli law

| Question | Israeli law (Module 1) | International law |
|---|---|---|
| Who makes the law? | The Knesset, ministers, the courts | States themselves, by treaty and by practice; no legislature above them |
| Who is bound? | Everyone in Israel | Mainly states (and international organisations); individuals mostly through their own state's law |
| Who enforces it? | Courts, police, execution office | Mostly self-help, diplomacy, reputation, and courts only where states consented |

The core idea is **consent**. A state is bound by a treaty because it agreed to it (lesson 13.2), and by custom because states generally practise a rule as law (lesson 13.3). That is why every international argument starts with the question: *did Israel accept this rule, and how?*

### The binding-or-persuasive test

Sort every international source you find into one of four boxes:

| Box | Examples | Weight in an Israeli letter or petition |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Binds Israel as a treaty** | ICCPR, CRC, CRPD, the Genocide Convention (Israel ratified all four) | Strong; but an Israeli court applies it only through Israeli law (lesson 13.5) |
| **2. Binds Israel as custom** | The Hague Regulations of 1907 on occupation; diplomatic immunity; the prohibition of torture | Strong; Israeli courts treat custom as part of Israeli law (lesson 13.5) |
| **3. Helps find the law** | ICJ judgments and advisory opinions; ILC conclusions; leading textbooks | Persuasive; cite with care, and say whose view it is |
| **4. Soft law and positions** | UN General Assembly resolutions; Special Rapporteur and treaty-body reports; NGO reports; a government's own statements | Not binding by itself; useful as evidence of practice or of an argument |

Two warnings:

- A Security Council resolution adopted under Chapter VII of the UN Charter can bind states; most General Assembly resolutions do not. Check the resolution's wording before you call it binding.
- Material in box 4 sometimes becomes box 2: repeated resolutions and state statements can be evidence of custom. That is an argument you have to make, not a fact you can assume.

### Who says what: Israel as an actor in international law

Israel takes part in making international law. Its Foreign Ministry publishes its legal positions on [israelihl.mfa.gov.il](https://israelihl.mfa.gov.il/general_international_law), including statements in the UN Sixth Committee on the work of the International Law Commission. In 2025, for example, Israel told the Committee it was concerned that draft articles might be read as customary law "when they are in fact mere proposals for progressive development". Human-rights NGOs, UN bodies and other states often take broader views of what the law requires. In this course you always attribute: "Israel's position is…", "the Committee's view is…", "the Court held…".

## Worked example

Rina chairs a small association that defends the right of Jewish and Muslim worshippers to pray at sites under municipal control. She drafts a letter to a city council and lists her international sources:

1. ICCPR Art. 18 (freedom of religion).
2. UN General Assembly Declaration on the Elimination of All Forms of Intolerance and of Discrimination Based on Religion or Belief (1981).
3. A Special Rapporteur's report criticising restrictions on worship in several countries.
4. An ICJ advisory opinion that mentions access to holy places.
5. A statement by Israel's Foreign Ministry on freedom of worship.

**Step 1 — Box each source.** (1) is a treaty Israel ratified in 1991 → box 1. (2) is a General Assembly declaration → box 4. (3) is a rapporteur's report → box 4. (4) is an advisory opinion → box 3; it is not binding, and Israel rejects some ICJ opinions, so she must attribute it. (5) is Israel's own position → box 4, but powerful in a letter to an Israeli authority, because it shows the State's own view.

**Step 2 — Lead with the strongest.** Her letter opens with ICCPR Art. 18 and the State's own statement, then uses the declaration as "the international standard", and cites the rapporteur only as an example.

**Step 3 — Check the Israeli route.** The ICCPR binds Israel internationally, but an Israeli court will apply it mainly as an aid to interpreting Israeli law (lesson 13.5). Rina keeps Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty (lesson 03.2) as her main legal ground and uses the ICCPR to support it.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your cause sentence from lesson 01.1 and your issue map (Module 1 project).

### Steps

1. Watch the Greenwood lecture (Learn more). Write down, in your own words, the three main sources and the two subsidiary means.
2. Search the web for five international texts people cite about your cause (from any side). Use at least one government source and one NGO or UN source.
3. For each, record: its name, who issued it, the date, and its box (1–4) from the binding-or-persuasive test.
4. For every box 1 source, find whether Israel signed and ratified it (the UN Treaty Collection; lesson 13.2 shows how).
5. Write two sentences: which source you would lead with in a letter to an Israeli authority, and why.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calling a General Assembly resolution "binding international law" | The authority dismisses the whole argument | Re-label it as soft law or as evidence of practice | Run the four-box test on every source |
| Quoting an ICJ advisory opinion as if it bound Israel | Loses credibility; the State answers that it rejects the opinion | Attribute it: "the Court's view is…", and add Israel's position | Remember Art. 59 and the difference between judgments and opinions |
| Citing a treaty Israel never ratified as binding on Israel | Wrong legal claim | Check the UN Treaty Collection; argue custom instead if you can | Check ratification before you write |
| Using only one side's sources | The letter reads as advocacy, not law | Add the government's position and the main counter-view | Always include at least one official Israeli source |

## Review

- International law is made mainly by states, by treaty and by custom; there is no world legislature.
- Article 38(1) lists treaties, custom and general principles as sources, and judicial decisions and scholarship as subsidiary means.
- ICJ decisions bind only the parties; only states can be parties in ICJ cases.
- Sort every source: treaty binding on Israel, custom, help to find the law, or soft law and positions.
- Attribute contested views, and include Israel's official position next to others.
