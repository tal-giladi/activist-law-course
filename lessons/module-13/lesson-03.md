# 13.3 · Customary International Law

Customary international law is unwritten law that binds states because they generally act in a certain way and believe they are legally required to. It matters a great deal in Israel, because Israeli courts treat custom as part of Israeli law without any statute. In this lesson you learn how custom is proven, how a state can stay outside a new rule, and which rules no state can escape, and you finish by building an evidence sheet for one customary rule.

## Why it matters

A treaty binds Israel only if Israel joined it, and an Israeli court applies it only through legislation (lesson 13.5). Custom is different: a customary rule binds Israel whether or not it signed anything, and an Israeli court can apply it directly. That makes "this rule is customary" one of the most powerful sentences in an international-law argument, and one of the most contested. Petitioners from the territories rely on the customary status of the Hague Regulations; the State argues that parts of the Fourth Geneva Convention are not customary; human-rights groups and governments argue over whether new rules have become custom. You need to know how such a claim is proven, so you can make it carefully and test the other side's.

## The law

### The two elements

> To determine the existence and content of a rule of customary international law, it is necessary to ascertain whether there is a general practice that is accepted as law (opinio juris).

*Gist:* custom needs both practice and the belief that the practice is law; one without the other is not enough (ILC conclusion 2).

> The relevant practice must be general, meaning that it must be sufficiently widespread and representative, as well as consistent.

*Gist:* not every state must follow the practice, but it must be widespread, representative and consistent (conclusion 8(1)).

> The requirement, as a constituent element of customary international law, that the general practice be accepted as law (opinio juris) means that the practice in question must be undertaken with a sense of legal right or obligation.

*Gist:* states must act because they think the law requires or permits it, not only out of courtesy or convenience (conclusion 9(1)).

### The persistent objector

> Where a State has objected to a rule of customary international law while that rule was in the process of formation, the rule is not opposable to the State concerned for so long as it maintains its objection.

*Gist:* a state that objected clearly and consistently while a rule was forming is not bound by it, as long as it keeps objecting (conclusion 15(1)).

Source for all four: [ILC Conclusions on identification of customary international law (2018)](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf). The General Assembly took note of them; they are guidance from the UN's expert body, not a treaty.

### Rules no state can escape

> A treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law. For the purposes of the present Convention, a peremptory norm of general international law is a norm accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character.

*Gist:* a few rules, such as the prohibitions of genocide and torture, are so fundamental that no treaty or objection can set them aside. Source: [Vienna Convention, art. 53](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/1_1_1969.pdf).

### Custom written into Israeli law

> למדינה זרה תהא חסינות מפני סמכות השיפוט של בתי המשפט בישראל, למעט סמכות השיפוט בעניינים פליליים (בחוק זה – חסינות מפני סמכות שיפוט), בכפוף להוראות חוק זה.

*Gist:* a foreign state is immune from the civil jurisdiction of Israeli courts, subject to the exceptions in the law (commercial transactions, employment, torts in Israel and more). Source: [חוק חסינות מדינות זרות, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_חסינות_מדינות_זרות). The Knesset wrote down a rule that had been customary and that Israeli courts had applied as custom before 2008.

## How it works in practice

### Where evidence of custom comes from

| Evidence | Practice or opinio juris? | Where to find it |
|---|---|---|
| National legislation | Practice (and sometimes opinio juris) | Wikisource, other states' official gazettes |
| National court decisions | Practice | Versa, court websites |
| Official statements, diplomatic letters, UN speeches | Both | Foreign ministry sites, e.g. [israelihl.mfa.gov.il](https://israelihl.mfa.gov.il/general_international_law) |
| Military manuals, government legal positions | Both | Official publications; the ICRC customary IHL database collects them |
| Widely ratified treaties | Can reflect or crystallise custom | UN Treaty Collection |
| General Assembly resolutions | Mainly opinio juris, if adopted with wide support and legal wording | UN documents |
| ICJ judgments, ILC work, leading scholarship | Subsidiary means to identify the rule | icj-cij.org, legal.un.org |

For the law of armed conflict, the [ICRC Customary IHL database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl) lists 161 rules with the practice behind each. It is the ICRC's own study, not a text that states negotiated or adopted, and states do not all accept every rule in it. Use it as a starting point, check the practice it cites, and attribute it.

### Israel's position on identifying custom

In its 2025 statements to the UN Sixth Committee, Israel warned that drafts of the International Law Commission may be "mistakenly" relied on "as reflecting customary international law, when they are in fact mere proposals for progressive development", and asked the Commission to separate clearly the rules that reflect custom from proposals for new law. Many NGOs and some states favour a faster recognition of new rules, especially in human rights. Your job is to show the evidence, not to assert the conclusion.

### A five-question test for any "customary rule" claim

1. **What exactly is the rule?** One sentence, as precise as a statute.
2. **Practice:** which states do this, and is the practice widespread, representative and consistent?
3. **Opinio juris:** do they say they do it because the law requires it?
4. **Contrary practice:** who acts otherwise, and do they justify it as an exception (which supports the rule) or reject the rule?
5. **Israel's position:** has Israel accepted, applied, or objected to the rule, and since when?

### Custom in the Israeli courts, in one paragraph

The Supreme Court has held many times that customary international law is part of Israeli law unless a Knesset statute contradicts it (lesson 13.5). For the territories, it has treated the Hague Regulations of 1907 as customary. In *Ajuri* (2002) it noted that the State disputes that the Fourth Geneva Convention as a whole is customary, while the State declared it applies the Convention's humanitarian provisions, and the Court ruled on that basis without deciding the question. That is how a customary-law argument works in practice: the parties' positions on the rule's status are themselves part of the argument.

## Worked example

Lina is an Israeli citizen who worked for eight years as a cleaner at a foreign embassy in Tel Aviv. She was dismissed without severance pay. The embassy's lawyer writes: "A sovereign state is immune from Israeli courts."

**Step 1 — The rule.** The embassy invokes state immunity, a customary rule. Lina's question is whether custom gives immunity for employment disputes with local staff.

**Step 2 — Practice and opinio juris.** Many states' laws and courts apply "restrictive" immunity: a state is immune for acts of government, but not for commercial acts or ordinary employment of local staff. They do so as a matter of law, not courtesy.

**Step 3 — Israel's codification.** The Foreign State Immunity Law 2008 grants immunity in s. 2, but s. 4 removes it for a claim by an employee if the claim belongs to the Labour Court, the work was done in Israel, and the employee was an Israeli citizen or resident (the section has further conditions and exceptions; read it in full).

**Step 4 — Decision.** Lina's claim fits s. 4(a). Her letter answers the embassy: "Under section 4 of the Foreign State Immunity Law, which reflects the restrictive doctrine of state immunity, a foreign state has no immunity in this claim." She files in the Regional Labour Court (lesson 11.1 on whether to go to court).

**What the example shows.** Before 2008 an Israeli court would have answered the same question by identifying the customary rule itself. Today the statute governs, and the customary rule helps to interpret it.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your source table from lesson 13.1 and treaty check from lesson 13.2.
- The [ICRC Customary IHL database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl) or the [ILC texts](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/draft_articles/1_13_2018.pdf), depending on your cause.
- 35 minutes for the assigned lecture, 15 minutes in the ICRC database and 40 minutes for the evidence sheet.

### Steps

1. Choose one rule that someone in your cause claims is customary. If your cause has none, use one of these: diplomatic premises are inviolable; a state is responsible for acts of its officials; an occupying power must restore and ensure public order and safety.
2. Write the rule as one precise sentence.
3. Find three items of practice (laws, court decisions, official statements) from at least two different regions of the world.
4. Find one statement showing opinio juris: a state saying it acts because the law requires it.
5. Find any contrary practice or objection, including Israel's position if there is one.
6. Run the five-question test and write a conclusion of three sentences: "The rule is / is probably / is not customary, because…", attributing every contested view.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asserting "this is customary law" with no evidence | The State denies it and you have nothing to answer | Add practice and opinio juris with sources | Use the five-question test before writing |
| Using only NGO or UN statements as evidence of practice | Weak: custom is built mainly from what states do and say | Add legislation, court decisions and official statements | Collect state evidence first |
| Ignoring Israel's own position | The court hears it first from the State | Quote it and answer it | Search the MFA legal site and Israeli rulings |
| Treating the ICRC study or ILC drafts as binding texts | The other side shows they are studies or proposals | Present them as evidence and attribute them | Label every source by type |
| Calling a disputed rule jus cogens to avoid proving it | Overreach that damages credibility | Prove it as ordinary custom first | Reserve jus cogens for clearly accepted cases |

## Review

- Custom needs a general practice and its acceptance as law; neither is enough alone.
- Practice must be widespread, representative and consistent; evidence comes from what states do and say.
- A persistent objector is not bound by a new rule while it keeps objecting, but jus cogens binds everyone.
- Israel codified foreign state immunity in 2008; custom also binds Israel without a statute.
- Always state Israel's position and attribute studies, drafts and NGO views.
