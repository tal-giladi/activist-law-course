# 13.2 · Treaties

A treaty is a written agreement between states that binds them under international law: the ICCPR, the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Genocide Convention are all treaties that Israel joined. In this lesson you learn how a state becomes bound, what reservations do, how treaties are read, and how Israel's Government and Knesset handle them, and you finish by tracing one treaty from the UN register to the Israeli statute book.

## Why it matters

"Israel signed the convention" is one of the most common claims in activist letters, and one of the most often wrong. Signing is not ratifying; ratifying is not the same as making the treaty part of Israeli law; and a reservation may take the exact article you rely on out of Israel's obligations. A disability activist citing the CRPD, a parents' group citing the Hague Child Abduction Convention and a security-minded group citing a counter-terrorism treaty all need the same three facts: did Israel ratify, with what reservations, and did the Knesset legislate it in.

## The law

### What a treaty is (Vienna Convention, Art. 2(1))

> (a) "treaty" means an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation;

*Gist:* the name ("covenant", "protocol", "agreement") does not matter; what matters is a written agreement between states governed by international law.

> (d) "reservation" means a unilateral statement, however phrased or named, made by a State, when signing, ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or to modify the legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that State;

*Gist:* a reservation can take a provision out of a state's obligations, which is why you must read the reservations before you cite an article.

### Signing already has an effect (Art. 18)

> A State is obliged to refrain from acts which would defeat the object and purpose of a treaty when: (a) it has signed the treaty or has exchanged instruments constituting the treaty subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, until it shall have made its intention clear not to become a party to the treaty;

*Gist:* a state that signed but did not ratify is not bound by every article, but must not defeat the treaty's purpose.

### Binding force and internal law (Arts 26–27)

> Every treaty in force is binding upon the parties to it and must be performed by them in good faith.

> A party may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty.

*Gist:* a state cannot escape a treaty by pointing to its own laws; internationally, the treaty wins.

### How to read a treaty (Art. 31(1))

> A treaty shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose.

*Gist:* start with the ordinary words, read them in context, and check the treaty's purpose.

Source for all four: [Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties 1969](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/1_1_1969.pdf). Israel is not a party to the Vienna Convention itself (it is not listed on the [UN status page](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/MTDSG/Volume%20II/Chapter%20XXIII/XXIII-1.en.pdf)); many of its rules are widely treated as customary law, and Israel's own UN statements refer to its rules, such as pacta sunt servanda.

### Who joins treaties for Israel

> הממשלה מוסמכת לעשות בשם המדינה, בכפוף לכל דין, כל פעולה שעשייתה אינה מוטלת בדין על רשות אחרת.

*Gist:* the Government may act for the state in anything the law does not give to another body; this residual power is the basis for signing and ratifying treaties. Source: [חוק-יסוד: הממשלה, s. 32](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הממשלה).

> בהתאם לסעיף 10 לתקנון עבודת הממשלה, שר המבקש להגיש הצעה להחלטת ממשלה בדבר אשרור אמנה בינ"ל, יעביר למזכירות הכנסת שבועיים קודם שני עותקים מנוסחה בתרגום לעברית ואת עיקריה כדי שיובאו לידיעת חברי הכנסת

*Gist:* a minister who wants the Government to ratify a treaty first sends the Knesset its Hebrew text and summary, two weeks ahead, for MKs' information.

> אין חובה שהממשלה תביא לאישור הכנסת כל אמנה בינ"ל עליה היא חתמה.

*Gist:* the Government need not bring every treaty to a Knesset vote; by convention it brings important political agreements. Source: [Knesset Legal Adviser, letter on ratification of international treaties, 3.5.2022](https://m.knesset.gov.il/About/Departments/Documents/Leg/LEG108.pdf), paras 2 and 8.

### When the Knesset makes a treaty Israeli law

> לסעיפי האמנה שנוסחם מובא בתוספת יהיה תוקף של חוק, והם יחולו על אף האמור בכל דין

*Gist:* this statute gives the articles of the Hague Child Abduction Convention the force of law in Israel, overriding any other law. Source: [חוק אמנת האג (החזרת ילדים חטופים), התשנ״א–1991, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_אמנת_האג_(החזרת_ילדים_חטופים)).

## How it works in practice

### The life of a treaty, step by step

| Step | International level | In Israel |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Negotiation and adoption | States agree a text, often at the UN | Foreign Ministry and Justice Ministry lawyers |
| 2. Signature | The state approves the text; Art. 18 duty begins | A Government decision, usually signed by a minister or ambassador |
| 3. Ratification or accession | The state deposits its instrument; now it is bound | Government decision after the text is laid before the Knesset for two weeks (Government regulations s. 10); reservations decided then |
| 4. Entry into force | For the state, on the date the treaty sets | — |
| 5. Domestic effect | Not an international question (Art. 27) | Only through a Knesset statute or regulations, or as an aid to interpreting Israeli law (lesson 13.5) |
| 6. Reporting | Human-rights treaties: periodic reports to a committee (Module 14) | Ministries prepare the State report |

The two-week period is a window for MKs: urgent questions, motions for the agenda and committee debates (Knesset Legal Adviser, 2022, para 7). In 2022 MKs from several parties used it to raise questions about the Council of Europe's Istanbul Convention on violence against women. Some urged Israel to sign; others objected. That is an example of how activists on any side can engage before ratification: through MKs (Module 12).

### Reading a treaty's status on the UN Treaty Collection

1. Find the treaty's chapter number. Human-rights treaties are in Chapter IV: IV-3 ICESCR, IV-4 ICCPR, IV-8 CEDAW, IV-9 CAT, IV-11 CRC, IV-15 CRPD; the Genocide Convention is IV-1.
2. Open the status page or its PDF version (for example [ICCPR, IV-4](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/MTDSG/Volume%20I/Chapter%20IV/IV-4.en.pdf)).
3. In the participants table, find Israel: the signature date and the ratification or accession date. For the ICCPR: signed 19 December 1966, ratified 3 October 1991.
4. Scroll to "Declarations and Reservations" and find ISRAEL. For the ICCPR, Israel reserved on Art. 23 that "matters of personal status are governed in Israel by the religious law of the parties concerned" and that it may apply that law where it is inconsistent with the Covenant.
5. Check "Objections" and the notes for later withdrawals or notifications. Israel also notified a derogation under ICCPR Art. 4 based on the state of emergency in force since 1948, relevant to Art. 9 (detention).

### Treaty or not? Non-binding instruments

Memorandums of understanding, joint declarations and political commitments are often not treaties at all. Israel told the UN Sixth Committee in 2025 that non-legally binding texts "cannot give rise to the principle of good faith or to pacta sunt servanda" and cannot create new customary rules. Others argue that such texts can still carry legal effects. When you cite an "agreement", check whether the parties meant it to be binding.

### Interpreting a treaty article

Use Art. 31: the ordinary words, their context (the whole treaty, its preamble, related agreements), and its object and purpose. Treaty bodies' General Comments (Module 14) are influential interpretations, but they are not binding on states; Israel and other states sometimes reject them. Attribute them.

## Worked example

Yossi, who uses a wheelchair, campaigns for accessible public transport in his town. He wants to cite the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) in a letter to the Ministry of Transport.

**Step 1 — Status.** On the UN Treaty Collection (IV-15) he finds Israel: signed 30 March 2007, ratified 28 September 2012. Israel is bound internationally.

**Step 2 — Reservations.** Under "Declarations and Reservations" he finds one Israeli reservation: on marriage in Art. 23(1)(a), to the extent that the personal-status laws of the religious communities do not conform. It does not touch accessibility, so he can rely on Art. 9.

**Step 3 — The right article.** CRPD Art. 9(1) requires States Parties to "take appropriate measures to ensure to persons with disabilities access, on an equal basis with others, to the physical environment, to transportation…". He applies Art. 31 of the Vienna Convention: the ordinary meaning covers buses and stations; the purpose is independent living.

**Step 4 — Israeli law.** On Wikisource he looks for a "חוק אמנת…" statute for the CRPD, like the one for the Hague Child Abduction Convention, and finds none. So the CRPD is not itself part of Israeli law. His legal ground stays the Equal Rights for Persons with Disabilities Law (lesson 09.4); the CRPD supports how that law should be read (lesson 13.5).

**Step 5 — The letter.** "Israel ratified the CRPD on 28 September 2012. Article 9 obliges States Parties to take appropriate measures to ensure access to transportation. The Equal Rights for Persons with Disabilities Law should be interpreted in light of this obligation."

2. On the UN Treaty Collection, record: Israel's signature date, ratification or accession date, and the full text of any reservation or declaration by Israel.
3. Mark whether any reservation touches the article you want to use.
4. Read the article you want to cite and write two sentences applying Art. 31: the ordinary meaning, and the purpose.
5. Search Wikisource and the Knesset legislation database for a statute that incorporates the treaty ("חוק אמנת…" or a statute that refers to it). Record what you find, or "none found".
6. Write one sentence for a letter, in the style of Yossi's step 5.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing "Israel signed" for a treaty Israel never ratified, as if it were bound | The authority corrects you and discounts the rest | Use the right term; argue Art. 18 or custom if it fits | Read the dates on the UN record |
| Citing an article Israel reserved on | Your argument is answered in one line | Acknowledge the reservation; argue around it or use another article | Read "Declarations and Reservations" first |
| Treating a ratified treaty as an Israeli statute | A court will not enforce it directly | Present it as an aid to interpretation (lesson 13.5) | Check for an incorporating statute |
| Quoting a committee's General Comment as binding | The State replies that it does not accept it | Attribute it and present it as persuasive | Separate the treaty text from its interpreters |
| Calling an MoU or joint declaration a treaty | Wrong legal claim | Check the parties' intention to be bound | Look at the text, form and registration |

## Review

- A treaty is a written agreement between states governed by international law, whatever its name.
- Signing creates only the Art. 18 duty; ratification or accession makes the state bound; reservations can change what binds it.
- In Israel the Government ratifies under its residual power; the text is laid before the Knesset for two weeks; a Knesset vote is not required for every treaty.
- A treaty becomes part of Israeli law only through legislation, like the Hague Child Abduction Convention Law.
- Read treaties by ordinary meaning, context and purpose, and attribute every interpretation.
