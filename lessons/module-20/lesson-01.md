# 20.1 · Choosing a Cause and a Theory of Change

This is the first step of your capstone: one real campaign, in your own name, that you run from start to finish and write up as a case study. By the end of this lesson you will have chosen a cause that passes six practical filters, written a goal that names who must decide what and by when, and drawn a theory of change that explains why your actions should lead to that decision — with the assumptions you will test along the way.

## Why it matters

Most campaigns that fail were never designed to succeed. They start with an outrage ("the municipality is ignoring us") and jump straight to a tool ("let's petition the High Court"), without asking who can actually fix the problem, what would make that person act, and which legal lever reaches them. Twenty modules gave you the tools. The capstone tests whether you can choose and combine them.

A university LL.B. ends with a seminar paper that argues a legal question. This course ends with something closer to a clinic's year-end project: a real-world strategy, carried out and reviewed honestly. Choosing well at the start decides most of the outcome. A small, concrete, winnable goal that you pursue with four well-ordered tools teaches more — and changes more — than a grand goal you abandon after one letter.

## The law

Three rules shape which cause you can take on and how.

### You act in your own name

> הפעולות המנויות להלן, לא יעשה אותן דרך עיסוק, או בתמורה אף שלא דרך עיסוק, אלא עורך דין; ואלה הפעולות:
> (1) ייצוג אדם אחר וכל טיעון ופעולה אחרת בשמו לפני בתי משפט, בתי דין, בוררים וגופים ואנשים בעלי סמכות שיפוטית או מעין שיפוטית;

*Gist:* representing another person, drafting documents for them and giving them legal advice, as an occupation or for payment, are reserved to lawyers. ([חוק לשכת עורכי הדין, s. 20](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_לשכת_עורכי_הדין))

Your capstone therefore runs in your own name, or in the name of a group or amuta that has authorised you (lesson [18.4](../module-18/lesson-04.md)). If the cause is someone else's personal case, your role is to inform and refer, not to file for them.

### The clocks start before you are ready

> (ב) לא נקבע מועד כאמור, תוגש העתירה בלא שיהוי, לפי נסיבות הענין, ולא יאוחר מארבעים וחמישה ימים מיום שההחלטה פורסמה כדין, או מיום שהעותר קיבל הודעה עליה או מיום שנודע לעותר עליה, לפי המוקדם.

*Gist:* a petition against an administrative decision must be filed without delay and within the deadline counted from the earliest of publication, notice or knowledge of the decision. ([תקנות בתי משפט לענינים מינהליים (סדרי דין), reg. 3(ב)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/תקנות_בתי_משפט_לענינים_מינהליים_%28סדרי_דין%29#סעיף_3))

The current deadlines are on [Current Numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md). If the decision you want to fight was made months ago, court may already be closed; your campaign then aims at a new decision, not the old one.

### A complaint does not stop a court clock

> (2) אין בהם כדי למנוע מהמתלונן או מאדם אחר להשתמש בזכות או לבקש סעד שהוא זכאי להם, אולם אם נקבע לכך מועד בחיקוק, לא יוארך המועד על ידי הגשת התלונה או בירורה.

*Gist:* an ombudsman complaint does not extend any statutory deadline. ([חוק מבקר המדינה, s. 45(א)(2)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_מבקר_המדינה#סעיף_45))

Every soft tool you plan must sit inside the hard deadlines, never after them.

## How it works in practice

### The capstone in one paragraph

You choose one cause. You plan a campaign that uses at least four different tools from at least three families in the course (information, oversight, pre-litigation and litigation, Knesset and local government, public communication, international). You carry out at least three real actions in your own name. You keep an execution log. At the end you write a case study and hand the campaign over. You may build on an action from Module 19 — a real FOI campaign (19.2), an oversight complaint (19.3), a Knesset or council step (19.4), an international submission (19.6) — but the capstone must be one planned campaign with one goal, not a collection of separate exercises.

The details and the rubric are in the project brief: [Module 20 project: Capstone campaign](../../projects/m20-capstone.md).

### The six filters

Write down two or three candidate causes, then score each one.

| Filter | Question | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Real | Is there a concrete, documented problem now? | It is a general opinion ("the system is unfair") with no specific decision or failure |
| 2. Yours | Can you act in your own name, or for a group that authorised you? | Only another person can complain or sue, and you would be acting for them |
| 3. Decided by someone | Is there a person or body with the power to fix it? | Nobody holds the power, or it is a private dispute with no public angle |
| 4. Legally hooked | Is there a rule, duty or right you can point to? | You cannot name a statute, regulation, procedure or ruling (Module 2) |
| 5. Timely | Are the clocks still open, and is there a decision point ahead? | Every deadline passed and no new decision is expected |
| 6. Within capacity | Can you give it the hours and money for 3–9 months? | It needs a costly lawsuit, a lawyer you cannot pay, or more time than you have |

Choose the cause that passes all six. A cause that passes five can work if you fix the failing filter first (for example, by forming a group to solve filter 2, or by narrowing the goal to solve filter 6).

### From problem to goal

A goal names the **decision-maker**, the **decision** and a **date**:

- Problem: "The council's buses do not stop near our neighbourhood."
- Weak goal: "Better public transport."
- Campaign goal: "By 30 June 2027, the municipality's transport department adds a stop on Herzl Street to line 12, or gives a reasoned written refusal we can challenge."

The second half of that goal matters. A reasoned refusal is not a failure: it is a decision you can challenge (lesson [04.4](../module-04/lesson-04.md)), and it is a result you control.

Find the decision-maker with lesson [04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md): the official or body the law gives the power to, not the most visible person.

### Building the theory of change

A theory of change is the chain of "if … then … because" that links what you will do to the decision you want. Build it backwards:

1. **Long-term goal.** The decision you want, as written above.
2. **Preconditions.** What must be true just before the decision-maker acts? Usually two to four of these: the decision-maker *knows* the facts; is *legally required* or *pressured* to decide; has *no good reason* to refuse; and sees a *cost* in refusing (a court, an oversight finding, a council vote, public attention).
3. **Actions.** For each precondition, which tool from the course creates it? Facts come from FOI and documentation; legal duty from a formal letter and a pre-petition letter; oversight from a complaint; political cost from a council question or a committee session; public attention from the media (lesson 20.4).
4. **Assumptions.** What are you assuming that might be false? "The data exists." "The council member will ask the question." "The decision is in the First Schedule." Each assumption becomes something you check early.
5. **Indicators.** How will you know a precondition is in place? "FOI answer received with the ridership data." "Item on the council agenda."

Write it as one diagram or one table. Keep it on one page; you will update it in lesson 20.3 when reality disagrees.

### Two legal levers, and a shield

Law serves a campaign in three ways. Know which one you are using at each step:

- **Lever on the decision-maker:** a duty to decide, to give reasons, to hear you, to release information. Formal letters, FOI and pre-petition letters use it.
- **Lever through a third party:** a court, an oversight body, the Knesset, a UN mechanism that can make the decision-maker act or explain.
- **Shield for you:** the rights that protect your protest and speech (Module 6), your defences against defamation claims (Module 8), and the rules that keep you out of criminal trouble (Module 7).

### Neutral tools, your cause

The course does not tell you which cause to choose. It does ask that your case study describes the other side's position fairly and attributes every contested claim (plan §5). A campaign that understands the opposing argument writes better letters and loses fewer cases.

## Worked example

**Situation.** Shira lives in Kiryat Shmona, near the northern border. Two of the municipal kindergartens in her neighbourhood have no protected space nearby, and during alerts the staff move the children to a building across the road. She wants to choose a capstone cause.

1. **Candidates.** (a) "Shelters in the north are underfunded." (b) "The two kindergartens on her street need protected spaces." (c) "Her child's kindergarten teacher was rude to her."
2. **Filters.** (a) fails filters 3 and 6: too broad, many decision-makers, beyond her capacity. (c) fails filter 1 for a campaign: it is a personal complaint (lesson 19.3 territory), not a public cause. (b) passes: real and documented (she photographs the route and times the walk), hers (her child attends; other parents will sign in their own names), decided by someone (the municipality builds and the Home Front Command sets standards — she checks who holds which power), legally hooked (she researches the civil-defence legislation and the relevant standards in a research memo, Module 2), timely (the municipal budget for next year is being prepared), within capacity.
3. **Goal.** "By the approval of the 2027 municipal budget, the municipality includes protected spaces, or an interim solution approved by the Home Front Command, for the two kindergartens on Ha-Shoshanim Street, or gives a reasoned written decision explaining why not."
4. **Theory of change, backwards.**
   - Precondition A: the municipality has the facts in writing → formal letter with photos and timings (02.5).
   - Precondition B: the public knows what the standard requires and what was planned → FOI request for the inspection reports and the shelter survey (05.2).
   - Precondition C: the council must discuss it → a council member submits a question (12.5).
   - Precondition D: refusal has a cost → a pre-petition letter if the answer is unreasoned (05.5); local media coverage (20.4).
   - Assumptions: "an inspection report exists" (test: FOI); "the matter is a municipal decision, not only a ministry's" (test: research and the municipality's answer); "a council member will ask" (test: two meetings in the first month).
5. **Name.** Shira acts in her own name; the parents' committee decides to sign a joint letter, each parent in their own name.

**Mirror test.** The same filters and the same backwards map work for parents in an unrecognised Bedouin village in the Negev seeking protected spaces for a school, for a Tel Aviv group seeking a crossing guard at a dangerous junction, or for a Haredi neighbourhood seeking a ritual-bath accessibility upgrade. Only the facts and the decision-maker change.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your issue maps and memos from earlier projects (m01, m02, m04, m05), and your Module 19 portfolio if you have one.
- A calendar for the next 3–9 months.
- About 2 hours.

### Steps

1. List two or three candidate causes, each in one sentence.
2. Score each against the six filters in the table above, with one line of evidence per filter.
3. Choose one. Write down why the others lost.
4. Write the goal: decision-maker, decision, date, and "or a reasoned written decision".
5. Name the decision-maker and the section of law that gives them the power (lesson 04.1).
6. Map backwards: two to four preconditions, at least one action per precondition, each linked to the lesson that teaches it.
7. List at least three assumptions, and for each, the earliest action that will test it.
8. Write in whose name you act. If a group, record its decision authorising the campaign. Write where you stop and refer (lesson 18.4).
9. Write the other side's strongest argument in two sentences, attributed.

### Template

Use [Theory of change](../../templates/theory-of-change.md). It has the six-filter table, the goal sentence, the backwards map and the assumptions list.

### How you know it worked

- A stranger reading your goal can say who must decide what, and by when.
- Every precondition has an action, every action has a lesson, and every assumption has a test.
- You can explain in one sentence why the cause passes filter 2 (yours) and filter 5 (timely).

### Self-check

- [ ] Two or three candidates scored against six filters.
- [ ] Goal with decision-maker, decision, date and "or a reasoned decision".
- [ ] Decision-maker and the source of their power named.
- [ ] Backwards map: preconditions, actions, lessons.
- [ ] Three assumptions, each with an early test.
- [ ] In whose name, and where the campaign stops.
- [ ] The other side's argument, fairly stated.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| A goal with no decision-maker ("raise awareness") | No way to tell success from failure; the campaign drifts | Rewrite the goal as a decision by a named body | Use the goal formula from the start |
| Choosing a cause whose deadlines have passed | Court is closed; the authority ignores you | Aim at the next decision point (budget, plan, renewal) | Check filter 5 before anything else |
| Taking up another person's personal case | Acting for another, possibly a reserved act under s. 20 | Turn it into a group cause or refer the person | Apply filter 2; read lesson 18.4 |
| A theory of change with no assumptions | You learn too late that the data or the power does not exist | Add assumptions and test them first | Ask "what must be true?" for each step |
| A goal too big for 3–9 months | Burnout and abandonment | Narrow to one decision, one place | Filter 6, honestly scored |
| Starting with the most dramatic tool | Petition dismissed for not exhausting remedies; credibility lost | Go back to the letter and the FOI request | Order tools from soft to hard (lesson 20.2) |

## Review

- The capstone is one real campaign, in your own name, with at least four tools from three families and at least three real actions.
- A good cause is real, yours, decided by someone, legally hooked, timely and within your capacity.
- A goal names the decision-maker, the decision and the date, and counts a reasoned written refusal as a result.
- Build the theory of change backwards: goal, preconditions, actions, assumptions, indicators.
- Hard deadlines govern; complaints and soft tools never extend them (State Comptroller Law s. 45).
