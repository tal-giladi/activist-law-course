# 17.2 · Interviews and Informed Consent

A testimony is the heart of most human-rights documentation, and the part most likely to hurt someone if it is done badly. In this lesson you learn when not to interview, how to get informed consent that really is informed, how to run a short trauma-informed interview remotely or in a safe place, and how to record and store it — and you prepare a full interview plan and a bilingual consent form.

> [!WARNING]
> Interviewing people about violence can re-traumatise them and can expose them to retaliation. This course does **not** train you to interview children, survivors of sexual violence or people in acute crisis: refer them to trained professionals and specialised organisations. Interview only people who are safe where they are, by video or phone or in a safe public or private place — never in a conflict zone, a closed area or during an ongoing event. If someone is in danger now, the first call is to emergency services, not to a documenter. This lesson is not legal advice and not psychological training.

## Why it matters

A testimony gives the facts no video can show: what was said, how long it lasted, what happened afterwards, who else was there. It is also personal information about someone at their most vulnerable. A survivor who is pressed to tell the story four times to four organisations, who discovers her name in a public report, or whose neighbour learns she spoke out, is harmed a second time — and the next victim will not talk. Courts and UN bodies also look at how a testimony was taken: leading questions, payments, promises or pressure make it worthless. Consent and care are not extras; they are what make the testimony usable.

## The law

> Informed consent requires disclosure of all material information – including the purpose of the evaluation – potential risks and benefits, the nature of the evaluation – including the possibility of taking photographs – limits on confidentiality …, how information gathered in the evaluations will be used and stored and who will have access to the information.

*Gist:* consent is "informed" only if the person knows the purpose, the risks and benefits, what will happen (including photos), the limits of confidentiality, how the material will be used and stored, and who will see it. Written for clinicians, the rule is the standard for every documenter. ([Istanbul Protocol 2022, para. 167](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/publications/2022-06-29/Istanbul-Protocol_Rev2_EN.pdf))

> Consent should be confirmed once again at the end of the interview after the disclosure of specific information by the alleged victim …

*Gist:* people often say more than they planned; ask again at the end whether they still agree, now that they know what they said. (Istanbul Protocol, para. 168)

> Interviews that are not properly conducted may retraumatize victims, place them at additional risk, affect the quality and reliability of the information provided and distort victims' memory of the events.

*Gist:* a badly run interview harms the person and the evidence; the Protocol also tells investigators to avoid duplicate interviews of the same victim. (Istanbul Protocol, paras 205, 210)

> ”האזנת סתר“ – האזנה ללא הסכמה של אף אחד מבעלי השיחה;

*Gist:* recording an interview you take part in is not wiretapping, so it is lawful — but ethically, you record only with the interviewee's explicit consent, which you ask for on the recording itself. ([Wiretap Law, s. 1](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_האזנת_סתר#סעיף_1); lesson [09.5](../module-09/lesson-05.md))

> (8) הפרה של חובת סודיות לגבי עניניו הפרטיים של אדם, שנקבעה בהסכם מפורש או משתמע;

*Gist:* if you promised confidentiality, breaking that promise is an invasion of privacy; s. 2(9) adds that information given for one purpose may not be used for another. Promise only what you can keep. ([Privacy Law, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_הגנת_הפרטיות#סעיף_2))

## How it works in practice

### Before: should this interview happen at all?

Ask four questions first. If any answer is "no", do not interview — refer or wait.

1. **Is it needed?** Does the testimony add something your purpose needs (17.1), or does it repeat what is already documented? If another organisation has already interviewed the person, ask them for the material, with the person's consent, instead.
2. **Is it safe?** For the person (could anyone find out and retaliate?), for their family, for you. Is the person somewhere private and safe right now?
3. **Is the person ready and free to choose?** No pressure from family, community or your group; no payment or promise in return; the person is not in acute distress.
4. **Are you the right person?** Children, survivors of sexual violence, people who were tortured, and people in acute crisis need trained interviewers. The Istanbul Protocol requires prior training for such interviews; the Murad Code sets standards for anyone gathering information about conflict-related sexual violence. Refer them to specialised organisations and help lines (below).

### The consent conversation

Consent is a conversation before it is a signature. Use plain language, in the person's language (with an interpreter if needed), and cover:

- **Who you are** and who you are not: not police, not a lawyer acting for them, not a therapist.
- **The purpose** and every planned use: a complaint, a petition, a UN submission, a public report, an archive.
- **Who will see it:** your group, a lawyer, the police, a UN body, the Government of Israel (in a UN communication, 14.4), the public.
- **Identity:** named, initials, pseudonym, or code only; face and voice shown, blurred or not recorded.
- **Recording:** notes only, audio or video. Ask separately for each.
- **Risks:** retaliation, being recognised, being called as a witness, painful memories.
- **No promises:** you cannot promise a result, compensation, an arrest or a news story.
- **The right to stop, skip any question, and withdraw** — and the honest limit: once something is published or filed, it cannot be fully withdrawn.
- **Storage:** where it is kept, for how long, who has access, and when it is deleted.

Record the consent in writing (the bilingual [interview-consent-form.md](../../templates/interview-consent-form.md)) or on the recording. For people who cannot read the form, read it aloud and record their oral agreement. Repeat the key points at the end of the interview.

**Minors.** Do not interview children about violence in this course. If a young person (under 18) is a witness to something non-violent, you need a parent's or guardian's consent and the young person's own agreement, and an adult they trust in the room.

### During: trauma-informed practice

The principles commonly used are safety, trust, choice, collaboration and empowerment (cited in the Istanbul Protocol, note 357).

- **Set up.** A quiet, private space; for a video call, ask who else is in the room and whether they can speak freely; agree on a sign for "stop". Turn off notifications. Plan at most 45–60 minutes.
- **Begin with a free account.** "Please tell me, in your own words, what happened on that day." Do not interrupt.
- **Then clarify with open questions.** "What happened next?" "What could you see from where you were?" "How do you know it was about ten o'clock?"
- **Avoid leading questions.** Not "Did the officer hit you with a baton?" but "What did the officer do?" Not "Were they settlers?" but "Who were the people? What did they look like?"
- **Separate what they saw from what they heard from others.** "Did you see that yourself, or did someone tell you?"
- **Do not share other testimonies** or show other videos before the account is complete; it changes memory.
- **Watch for distress:** silence, shaking, avoidance, sudden tiredness. Offer a break, change the subject, or stop. Never push for details that are not needed for the purpose.
- **Do not judge, argue or show doubt.** Note inconsistencies quietly for later corroboration; memory of trauma is often fragmented.
- **Interpreters** translate exactly, in first person, and keep confidentiality too.

### After: closing, support and storage

1. Ask: "Is there anything else you want to add? Anything you want me *not* to use?"
2. Re-confirm consent and identity choices now that the person knows what they said.
3. Give support contacts (checked 13.10.2026): ERAN emotional first aid **1201** (also chat and WhatsApp); NATAL trauma helpline **\*3362** (24/7, for those affected by war and terror); Rape Crisis Centers **1202** (women) and **1203** (men), with an Arabic line and chat. Lawyers and legal aid: [where-to-get-help](../../references/where-to-get-help.md).
4. Tell the person what happens next and when you will be in touch. Keep that promise.
5. Within 24 hours, write a short note: date, time, method, who was present, interpreter, consent given, anything that affects reliability.
6. Store the recording and the note under the person's code, encrypted, separate from the name key (17.4, 17.5). Hash the original recording (17.4).
7. Look after yourself. Listening to trauma affects documenters too (vicarious trauma). Debrief with a colleague, limit the number of interviews per day, and use the same support lines if you need them.

## Worked example

**Situation.** Rami, an olive farmer from a village near Nablus, contacts a documentation volunteer, Noam Levi, through a Palestinian NGO that already took his first statement. He says masked men came from the direction of a nearby outpost, beat him and cut his trees. He wants an Israeli police complaint filed and his story told to Israeli readers.

1. **Is it needed?** Noam first asks the NGO for its statement, with Rami's consent. He needs only details missing for the complaint: time, number of people, vehicles, injuries, the police reply so far.
2. **Is it safe?** Rami fears his name will reach the attackers. Agreement: initials only in public; full name only in the police complaint, which he files himself with the NGO's help.
3. **Remote setup.** A Signal video call at a time Rami chooses, with an interpreter from the NGO; Rami is at home, his son in the room by his choice.
4. **Consent.** The interpreter reads the Arabic consent form; Rami agrees on the recording to an audio recording (not video), to the police complaint, and to a public report with initials; he refuses any photo of his face.
5. **Interview.** Free account first; then "How many people did you see?", "What were they wearing?", "Where did they come from — what could you see?" Rami says "they were settlers"; Noam asks "What made you think so?" and records the answer: they came on foot from the outpost's direction and left the same way.
6. **Close.** Consent re-confirmed; Rami asks to remove the name of a neighbour he mentioned. Noam gives the NGO's contact and the complaint steps. The audio is hashed and stored encrypted under code V-07.

**Mirror test.** The same steps fit a remote interview with a resident of a Gaza-border kibbutz about the army's slow response on 7 October for a state inquiry submission (with referral to NATAL for trauma), a Haredi yeshiva student about police conduct at a draft protest, or an Ethiopian-Israeli teenager's parent about a police stop. What changes is the language, the risks and the referral — not the method.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The template [interview-consent-form.md](../../templates/interview-consent-form.md).
- An adult friend or family member who agrees to a 15-minute practice interview about a non-traumatic event they witnessed (a minor traffic accident, a council meeting, a bureaucratic refusal).
- A phone that can record audio.
- 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write a one-page interview plan for an invented case on your project issue: purpose, the four "should this happen" questions with your answers, risks, referral options, and the five to eight open questions you will ask.
2. Fill the consent form for the practice interview, choosing what will be recorded and how the person is identified.
3. Hold the consent conversation with your friend, covering every point in the list above, and record their oral consent at the start of the audio.
4. Run the 15-minute interview: free account first, then open clarifying questions. Do not ask a single leading question.
5. Close it: anything to add or remove, consent re-confirmed, "what happens next".
6. Listen to the recording and mark every question that was leading or closed; rewrite each one as an open question.
7. Write the 24-hour note and store the audio under a code, then delete the practice recording when you are done (your friend's consent was for practice only).

### Template

- [interview-consent-form.md](../../templates/interview-consent-form.md) — consent form in Hebrew and English (with a place for an Arabic translation), covering purpose, uses, identity, recording, risks, withdrawal and storage, and a re-confirmation box for the end of the interview.

### How you know it worked

- Your friend can repeat, in their own words, what the recording will be used for and how to withdraw.
- The transcript has no leading questions, or you rewrote every one.
- Consent was confirmed at the start and again at the end.
- The stored file is named by code, not by name.

### Self-check

- [ ] I know when not to interview and whom to refer to.
- [ ] My consent conversation covers purpose, uses, audience, identity, recording, risks, no promises, withdrawal and storage.
- [ ] I start with a free account and use open questions.
- [ ] I re-confirm consent at the end and give support contacts.
- [ ] I keep only the confidentiality promises I can keep.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interviewing a person already interviewed by another group | Re-traumatisation; two inconsistent accounts | Stop; get the first statement with consent | Ask first who has documented the case |
| Consent only for "documentation", then publishing names | Privacy breach (s. 2(8), 2(9)); risk to the person | Remove the names; apologise; ask again | Ask separately for each use and for identity |
| Leading questions ("Did they beat you with a stick?") | The account is challenged as suggested | Re-interview only if essential, with open questions | Free account first; open questions |
| Promising a result or protection you cannot give | Broken trust; the person feels used | Correct it honestly at once | Say clearly what you cannot promise |
| Interviewing a child or a survivor of sexual violence without training | Serious harm; unusable evidence | Stop; refer to specialists | Use the "should this happen" check |
| Recording file named "Rami_attack_village.mp3" in a shared folder | A leak identifies the person | Rename to a code; move to encrypted storage | Codes and encryption from the first file |

## Review

- First decide whether the interview should happen at all: needed, safe, free and with the right interviewer; refer children, survivors of sexual violence and people in crisis to specialists.
- Informed consent covers purpose, uses, audience, identity, recording, risks, no promises, withdrawal and storage, and is confirmed again at the end (Istanbul Protocol paras 167–168).
- Trauma-informed practice: safety, trust, choice, collaboration and empowerment; a free account, then open questions; breaks; never push for details.
- Recording your own interview is lawful under the Wiretap Law, but record only with explicit consent; keep every confidentiality promise (Privacy Law s. 2(8)).
- Close with support contacts, a 24-hour note, coded and encrypted storage — and care for yourself.
