# 06.1 · Freedom of Expression and Assembly in Israeli Law

By the end of this lesson you will know where the right to protest comes from in Israeli law, the test the police and courts use before they may restrict a protest, and the difference between a lawful condition on time, place and manner and an unlawful ban on a message. You will finish by writing a short rights analysis of a protest you might organise or join.

## Why it matters

Every protest decision in Israel — a licence, a condition, a dispersal, an arrest — is measured against a short list of rules that no single statute spells out. The Police Ordinance dates from the British Mandate; the protection of protest was built by the High Court of Justice, starting with *Kol Ha'am* in 1953, and is now summarised in an Attorney General guideline that binds the police. If you know that list, you can tell a police officer, a municipality or a judge which rule they are applying, and you can spot when they are applying the wrong one.

The same rules protect every camp. The settlers' march against a disengagement, the anti-government rally on Kaplan Street, the Haredi protest against the draft, the Arab citizens' protest against crime and the hostages' families' vigil all rely on the same tests. This module teaches those tests; it never asks you which protest is right.

## The law

There is no Basic Law article that says "freedom of expression" or "freedom of assembly". The courts derived both from human dignity (see 03.4).

> אין פוגעים בחייו, בגופו, או בכבודו של אדם באשר הוא אדם.

*Gist:* the dignity clause, from which the Court derived the constitutional status of expression and protest. ([חוק־יסוד: כבוד האדם וחירותו, s. 2](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו))

> אין בחוק־יסוד זה כדי לפגוע בתקפו של דין שהיה קיים ערב תחילתו של חוק־היסוד.

*Gist:* laws older than 1992 — including the Police Ordinance's licensing rules — stay valid, but courts interpret them in the light of the Basic Law. ([s. 10](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו))

> מפקד המחוז, במתן רשיון לקיים הפגנה, אינו עושה חסד עם מבקש הרשיון, אלא מאפשר לו לממש זכות יסוד, ולפיכך מן הראוי שהמפקד ייתן רשיון כמבוקש, אלא אם קיימת רמת הסתברות קרובה לוודאי לפגיעה קשה ורצינית בבטחון הציבור או בסדר הציבורי

*Gist:* a licence is the exercise of a fundamental right, not a favour; it is granted as requested unless there is a near certainty of serious harm to public safety or order. ([AG guideline 3.1200, para. 13(א)](https://free-justice.openapi.gov.il/free/moj/portal/rest/searchpredefinedapi/v1/SearchPredefinedApi/Documents/LegalAdvisor/e9~GhUz~~oS7ArbJp93YY+iCnG5cZ2jRGdXIgwg+9PE=))

> העניין בגינו מבוקש הרשיון לקיים הפגנה, או נושא ההרצאה או הנאום, או הרקע האידיאולוגי של המארגנים או המשתתפים בהפגנה, ככלל, אינו עניין למשטרה ואין בו שיקול כדי לסרב רשיון

*Gist:* the protest's subject and the organisers' ideology are, as a rule, not the police's business and are not a reason to refuse. (AG guideline 3.1200, para. 13(ב))

> יודגש כי המשטרה רשאית להגביל את הזכות להפגין רק כאשר מתקיימת ודאות קרובה לפגיעה קשה ורצינית בסדר הציבורי או בשלום הציבור, ורק במידה הנדרשת על מנת למנוע פגיעה כאמור.

*Gist:* even then, the police may restrict only as much as needed, and must prefer conditions of place, time and manner to a ban. (AG guideline 3.1200, para. 13(ו))

> השר יתווה את מדיניות המשטרה ואת העקרונות הכלליים לפעילותה

*Gist:* since Amendment 37 (2022), the National Security Minister sets police policy and general principles. ([פקודת המשטרה, s. 8ג(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/פקודת_המשטרה#סעיף_8ג)) The High Court struck down the companion section 8ד (investigations policy) in HCJ 8987/22 (2.1.2025).

The leading rulings are free in English on Versa: [*Kol Ha'am* (HCJ 73/53)](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/kol-haam-co-ltd-v-minister-interior) — expression may be limited only on a near certainty of real harm; [*Levi* (HCJ 153/83)](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/levi-v-commander-southern-district-israeli-police) — a ban is a last resort, after the police have used reasonable means to protect the protest; and [*Majority Camp* (HCJ 2557/05)](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/majority-camp-v-israel-police) — the police may not make organisers pay for the policing of a protest.

## How it works in practice

### The two balances

| Balance | What is weighed | Test | Typical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical | Protest against public order, security, traffic | Near certainty of serious, real harm, based on concrete evidence (*Kol Ha'am*, *Levi*; AG guideline para. 13(ו)) | Conditions on route, hours, amplification; a ban only as a last resort |
| Horizontal | Protest against another person's right (privacy, property, freedom of movement) | Mutual concessions; time, place and manner | Distance from a private home, quiet hours, a route that keeps a hospital entrance open |

### What the police may and may not consider

- **May:** the size, the place, traffic, the hours, past experience with the same organisers, credible intelligence of violence, the forces available (only in special circumstances, and then to move the date or change conditions, not to ban).
- **May not:** the message, the organisers' politics, the fact that opponents are angry (the "heckler's veto" — the police must protect the lawful protest against those who threaten it, *Levi*; AG guideline paras. 16–17), the cost of policing as a charge on organisers (*Majority Camp*).
- **Public figures' homes** are a special case (the AG guideline, section ה): protests near the private home of an elected or senior official are allowed only with conditions of distance and quiet, and generally not at the home of a junior civil servant. 06.2 covers this.

### Who decides, and the 2023–2026 dispute

Licences and on-the-ground decisions are made by the **district commander** and the officers at the scene (Police Ordinance ss. 84–85; Penal Law s. 153). Since Amendment 37 (December 2022) the **National Security Minister** sets general police policy (s. 8ג).

How far that policy power reaches into protest policing has been contested since 2023. The positions are set out below; each is attributed:

- **The Minister** (Itamar Ben Gvir) has said his policy power includes protests. His August 2025 draft protest policy called for equal enforcement regardless of the protest's camp, and listed main roads that must never be blocked.
- **The Attorney General's office** told the Minister on 31.8.2025 that publishing the draft in that form would exceed his powers.
- **The High Court** has repeatedly held that the Minister may set general policy but may not give operational instructions on specific protests: in an interim order of January 2024, and in HCJ 8987/22 (2.1.2025).
- **The Israel Democracy Institute** published an opinion on 16.9.2025 calling the draft unlawful.
- **Petitions** concerning the Minister's role were still pending in 2026.

For you as an organiser, the practical point is that the decision about your protest is made by the police commander under the Ordinance and the AG guideline. Your letters and petitions go to the police, cite those rules, and copy the police legal adviser.

## Worked example

**Situation.** A right-wing group, "Forum for Victory", applies to march 2,000 people through central Tel Aviv on a Thursday evening in support of continuing the war. The district commander refuses, writing that "the march may lead to clashes with anti-government protesters who gather nearby" and that "the message is provocative to local residents". The organiser, Yair Ben-Ami, asks whether the refusal can stand.

1. **Is a licence needed?** A procession of 50 or more people needs one (Police Ordinance s. 83; AG guideline para. 8). Yes.
2. **Vertical balance.** The only reason given about harm is that *others* may clash with the marchers. *Levi* and AG guideline paras. 16–17 say the police must first use reasonable means to protect the lawful protest; a hostile audience is not, by itself, a reason to ban.
3. **The message.** "Provocative message" is a content-based reason; para. 13(ב) excludes ideology as a ground for refusal.
4. **Less harmful means.** A different route, a separation line from the other protest, an earlier hour — conditions under s. 85(2) — would answer the concern.
5. **Evidence.** The refusal cites no concrete intelligence of planned violence; the near-certainty test needs concrete facts.
6. **Conclusion.** The refusal is very likely unlawful. The next step is a written request for reconsideration with proposed conditions (06.2), and if refused, an urgent petition.
7. **Mirror test.** Replace the group with an anti-war group marching near a pro-government rally: every step and the conclusion stay the same. If your analysis would change, it is about the message, not the law.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- A real or realistic protest you might organise or join: its cause, place, expected size, date and form (vigil, rally, march, road block).
- The AG guideline 3.1200 (PDF linked in The law) and the Police Ordinance ss. 83–85.
- 40 minutes.

### Steps

1. Describe the protest in five lines: who, what, where, when, how many, and in what form.
2. List every interest that may conflict with it: traffic, residents, a hospital, a school, a public figure's home, an opposing group, security.
3. Sort each interest into the vertical or the horizontal balance.
4. For each, write the least restrictive condition that would answer it (route, hours, distance, sound level, separation from counter-protesters).
5. Write down what concrete evidence would justify a ban, and whether any exists.
6. Apply the mirror test: rewrite the protest with the opposite message and check that your analysis does not change.
7. Write the result as a one-page rights analysis. It becomes section 1 of your Module 6 project.

### How you know it worked

- Every restriction you foresee is linked to a specific interest and a specific, less restrictive condition.
- You can say, in one sentence each, why the message and the cost of policing are not reasons to refuse.
- The mirror test does not change your conclusions.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arguing the cause instead of the law in a letter to the police | The commander ignores it; the court sees no legal defect | Rewrite around the near-certainty test and less restrictive conditions | Leave the cause out of legal correspondence beyond one line |
| Accepting "your opponents may be violent" as a reason for a ban | You lose a protest the law protects | Cite *Levi* and AG guideline paras. 16–17; propose separation conditions | Ask for the concrete evidence behind every restriction |
| Treating a licence as a favour you must earn | You accept unnecessary conditions | Ask for each condition's reason and a less harmful alternative | Quote para. 13(א): a licence realises a fundamental right |
| Writing to the Minister about your protest's route | The Minister may not decide specific protests; the letter is wasted time | Write to the district commander, copy the police legal adviser | Route operational requests to the police |
| Analysing a protest you support more generously than one you oppose | Your argument fails when the other side uses it | Run the mirror test | Run the mirror test before you send anything |

## Review

- Freedom of expression and protest are constitutional rights derived from human dignity; the Mandate-era Police Ordinance stays valid but is read narrowly.
- The police may restrict a protest only on a near certainty of serious harm, based on concrete facts, and only as much as needed — conditions first, a ban last.
- The message, the organisers' politics, a hostile audience and the cost of policing are not reasons to refuse.
- Conflicts with other people's rights are solved with time, place and manner conditions.
- Decisions about your protest are made by the police commander under AG guideline 3.1200; the Minister's policy role is contested, and the courts have barred operational instructions on specific protests.
