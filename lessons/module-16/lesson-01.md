# 16.1 · International Crimes

By the end of this lesson you will be able to tell when the words "genocide", "crime against humanity" and "war crime" are being used as legal terms and when they are being used as slogans, test a described incident against the elements of each crime, and find the Israeli statute that defines the same crime.

## Why it matters

After 7 October 2023 and throughout the war in Gaza, every side used the vocabulary of international criminal law. Israeli officials called the Hamas attack genocide and crimes against humanity; Palestinian and international NGOs used the same words about Israel's conduct in Gaza; courts in The Hague and in Europe were asked to act on both. An activist who writes a complaint, a letter to the UN or a public statement needs to know what each word requires. A complaint that calls every civilian death "genocide" is dismissed in its first paragraph; one that names the crime, its elements and the facts that meet them is read to the end. This lesson gives you the definitions; lessons 16.2–16.5 show you the forums.

## The law

### The four crimes

> 1. The jurisdiction of the Court shall be limited to the most serious crimes of concern to the international community as a whole. The Court has jurisdiction in accordance with this Statute with respect to the following crimes:
> (a) The crime of genocide;
> (b) Crimes against humanity;
> (c) War crimes;
> (d) The crime of aggression.

*Gist:* international criminal law has four "core" crimes; anything else (terrorism, drug trafficking, torture outside these contexts) is prosecuted under national law and specific treaties. ([Rome Statute, art. 5(1)](https://legal.un.org/icc/statute/english/rome_statute%28e%29.pdf))

### Genocide: the special intent

> For the purpose of this Statute, "genocide" means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:

*Gist:* five listed acts (killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, conditions of life calculated to destroy it, preventing births, forcibly transferring children) become genocide only with the intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, as such. (Rome Statute, art. 6, copied from the 1948 Genocide Convention)

### Crimes against humanity: the context

> 1. For the purpose of this Statute, "crime against humanity" means any of the following acts when committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against any civilian population, with knowledge of the attack:

*Gist:* murder, extermination, deportation or forcible transfer, imprisonment in breach of fundamental rules, torture, rape and sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid and other inhumane acts are crimes against humanity when they are part of a widespread **or** systematic attack on civilians, in war or in peace. (Rome Statute, art. 7(1))

### War crimes: the armed-conflict link

> 1. The Court shall have jurisdiction in respect of war crimes in particular when committed as part of a plan or policy or as part of a large-scale commission of such crimes.

*Gist:* war crimes are serious violations of the law of armed conflict listed in art. 8(2): grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions (wilful killing, torture, hostage-taking…) and other serious violations (intentionally directing attacks against civilians, starvation as a method of warfare, pillage…). They need an armed conflict and a link to it. (Rome Statute, art. 8(1))

### Aggression

Art. 5(2) of the original Statute left the crime of aggression to be defined later. The Review Conference in Kampala adopted the definition on 11 June 2010 (resolution RC/Res.6), and the Court's jurisdiction over aggression is narrower than over the other three crimes ([UN Treaty Collection, XVIII-10-b](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=XVIII-10-b&chapter=18&clang=_en)). It is a leadership crime about the use of force by a state, and rarely relevant to activists' files.

### The same crimes in Israeli law

> בחוק זה ”השמדת עם“ פירושה – אחד המעשים המפורטים להלן שנעשה בכוונה להשמיד, השמדה גמורה או חלקית, קיבוץ לאומי, אתני, גזעי או דתי (להלן: ”קיבוץ“), באשר הוא קיבוץ כזה.

*Gist:* Israel's Genocide Law of 1950 copies the Convention definition, with the same special intent; the maximum penalty is death (s. 2). ([חוק בדבר מניעתו וענישתו של הפשע השמדת עם, ס׳ 1(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_בדבר_מניעתו_וענישתו_של_הפשע_השמדת_עם))

> (2) בתקופת השלטון הנאצי, בארץ עויינת, עשה מעשה שהוא בגדר פשע כלפי האנושות;
> (3) בתקופת מלחמת העולם השניה, בארץ עויינת, עשה מעשה שהוא בגדר פשע מלחמה,

*Gist:* the Nazis and Nazi Collaborators Law defines crimes against the Jewish people, crimes against humanity and war crimes, but only for the Nazi period and the Second World War; it is the law under which Adolf Eichmann was tried. ([חוק לעשיית דין בנאצים ובעוזריהם, ס׳ 1(א)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק_לעשיית_דין_בנאצים_ובעוזריהם))

Outside these two laws, Israel has no general statute defining war crimes or crimes against humanity. The Turkel Commission (2013) noted that the only explicit reference to "war crimes" in Israeli legislation is in the Nazis law, and that violations of IHL are prosecuted through ordinary offences in the Penal Law, the Military Justice Law and military orders ([Turkel II, Recommendation No. 1, para. 7](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/generalpage/downloads_eng1/en/ENG_turkel_eng_b1-474.pdf)). Lesson 16.3 follows what happened to its recommendation to legislate.

## How it works in practice

### The two-layer test

Every international crime has two layers. Check both before you use the word.

| Crime | Layer 1: the underlying act | Layer 2: the context or intent that must also be proven |
|---|---|---|
| Genocide | Killing, serious harm, destructive conditions of life, preventing births, transferring children | Intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group, in whole or in part, **as such** |
| Crime against humanity | Murder, deportation, torture, rape, persecution, imprisonment, disappearance… | A **widespread or systematic attack** on a civilian population, and the perpetrator's knowledge of it |
| War crime | Wilful killing, torture, hostage-taking, attacking civilians, starvation, pillage… | An **armed conflict** and a nexus between the act and the conflict |
| Aggression | Planning or executing an act of aggression by a state | A manifest violation of the UN Charter, by a person in a leadership position |

The same act can fit several crimes at once. Hostage-taking in an armed conflict is a war crime; if it is part of a widespread or systematic attack on civilians, it can also be a crime against humanity. Genocide additionally needs the special intent, which is the hardest element to prove and the one most often contested.

### What is *not* an international crime

- **A human-rights violation** (lesson 14.1) is a breach by a **state**; an international crime is an offence by an **individual**. Unreasonable detention conditions may breach the ICCPR without being a war crime.
- **An IHL breach** (lesson 15.1) is not always a war crime. A war crime needs a *serious* violation listed in the Statute and, for most, intent. A civilian death in an attack that was not excessive and not intended is not a crime at all under IHL.
- **An ordinary offence** (murder, assault) becomes an international crime only with the context in layer 2. Without it, it stays a domestic crime, investigated by the police or the military justice system (lesson 16.3).

### Where the definitions are applied

- **The ICC** applies the Rome Statute and its *Elements of Crimes* (lesson 16.2).
- **National courts** apply their own statutes. In Israel: the Genocide Law and the Nazis law, plus the Penal Law and the Military Justice Law for ordinary offences (lessons 16.3–16.4).
- **The ICJ** applies the Genocide Convention between **states**, not individuals (lesson 13.4); it does not convict anyone.
- **UN commissions of inquiry** (lesson 14.5) may say that conduct "may amount to" a crime; that is a finding, not a conviction.

### Who says what: the words in the current conflict

Contested labels must always be attributed. Examples of how to write them:

- "The Government of Israel describes the 7 October 2023 attack as crimes against humanity and war crimes, and its statement to the UN Sixth Committee on 11 October 2023 called them 'grave war crimes and crimes against humanity'."
- "The ICC Pre-Trial Chamber found, on 21 November 2024, reasonable grounds to believe that Mohammed Deif was responsible for crimes including murder, extermination and hostage-taking" (lesson 16.2).
- "The same Chamber found reasonable grounds to believe that Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant bear responsibility for the war crime of starvation and crimes against humanity; Israel rejects the Court's jurisdiction and the allegations" (lesson 16.2).

"Reasonable grounds to believe" is the standard for an arrest warrant. It is not a conviction, and your text must not say "committed".

## Worked example

**The situation.** A residents' group from a southern kibbutz and a human-rights group in Ramallah each send you a draft paragraph for an international submission. You are asked to check the legal labels, not the politics.

**Draft A (kibbutz group):** "On 7 October 2023 armed men entered the kibbutz, killed residents in their homes and abducted twelve, including children, to Gaza. This was genocide."

1. **Layer 1.** Killing and abduction of civilians: underlying acts for war crimes (wilful killing, hostage-taking), crimes against humanity (murder, imprisonment) and genocide (killing members of a group).
2. **Layer 2.** An armed conflict and nexus: arguable from the facts. A widespread or systematic attack on civilians: the draft should show scale and organisation (many communities, the same day, coordinated). Genocidal intent: the draft offers no facts on intent.
3. **Rewrite.** "The attack, carried out on the same morning against many communities, involved the killing and abduction of civilians, which may amount to war crimes and crimes against humanity. The group also alleges genocide and relies on [statements and documents showing intent]." The label stays; the elements are visible; the claim is attributed.

**Draft B (Ramallah group):** "Between March and May 2024 aid trucks were blocked and families in the north of Gaza had no food. This was the crime of starvation."

1. **Layer 1.** Art. 8(2)(b)(xxv) makes it a war crime to intentionally use starvation of civilians as a method of warfare, including by wilfully impeding relief supplies.
2. **Layer 2.** An armed conflict exists; the draft needs facts on **intent** and on who made which decision.
3. **Rewrite.** "The group alleges that relief supplies were wilfully impeded and relies on [dated records]. The ICC Pre-Trial Chamber found reasonable grounds to believe that this war crime was committed; Israel denies the allegations and rejects the Court's jurisdiction."

**Mirror test.** The same two-layer check applies to any label from any side: "pogrom" or "terror" for settler violence, "genocide" for Hamas's charter, "war crime" for a strike on a school. The legal test does not change with the cause.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- One public text that uses an international-crime label: a press release, an NGO report, a government statement or a UN statement, from any side.
- The [Rome Statute (PDF)](https://legal.un.org/icc/statute/english/rome_statute%28e%29.pdf), arts 6–8.
- The [international-law argument template](../../templates/intl-law-argument.md) (section on sources) or a blank page.
- 75 minutes.

### Steps

1. Copy the sentence that uses the label, with its source and date.
2. Write the underlying act(s) it describes (layer 1) and find the matching sub-paragraph in arts 6–8.
3. Write the context or intent required (layer 2) and list the facts in the text that support it, and the facts that are missing.
4. Decide: does the label fit, could it fit with more facts, or does it clearly not fit?
5. Rewrite the sentence in neutral, attributed language ("X alleges…", "the Chamber found reasonable grounds to believe…").
6. Repeat for one text from a source whose politics differ from the first.
7. Find the Israeli statute that defines the same crime, if any, and note one difference between it and the Rome Statute.

### Template

Use the "sources and weight" part of the [international-law argument template](../../templates/intl-law-argument.md). There is no separate template for this lesson.

### How you know it worked

- Each label is matched to an article and sub-paragraph of the Rome Statute.
- You can say in one line which element is the weakest.
- A reader cannot tell your own politics from your two rewrites.

### Self-check

- [ ] I checked both layers for each label.
- [ ] I did not write "committed" where only an allegation or a warrant exists.
- [ ] I separated state responsibility (ICJ, UN) from individual criminal responsibility (ICC, national courts).
- [ ] I analysed texts from two different sides.
- [ ] I found the Israeli statute, or noted that none applies.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calling every civilian death a "war crime" | The text is dismissed as advocacy; the real allegations are lost | Name the rule broken and the facts that show it (intent, excessiveness) | Run the two-layer test first |
| Saying "genocide" without facts on intent | The reader stops at the first unproven element | Add the intent evidence, or use a crime whose elements you can show | Treat genocidal intent as a separate element to prove |
| Writing "X committed" after a warrant or a UN finding | Misstates the law and invites a defamation complaint (lesson 08.1) | "The Chamber found reasonable grounds to believe…"; "the Commission found…" | Quote the standard the body used |
| Confusing the ICJ and the ICC | Wrong forum, wrong target (states vs individuals) | ICJ: states; ICC: individuals | Check who is the defendant |
| Assuming Israeli law has a general war-crimes offence | Wrong citation in a complaint | Cite the Penal Law or Military Justice Law offence, or the Genocide Law | Check lesson 16.3 and the Turkel recommendations |

## Review

- The four core crimes are genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression (Rome Statute art. 5).
- Each crime has an underlying act plus a context or intent: genocidal intent, a widespread or systematic attack on civilians, or an armed-conflict nexus.
- International crimes concern individuals; human-rights and ICJ cases concern states.
- Israel defines genocide in its 1950 Genocide Law and Nazi-era crimes in the Nazis and Nazi Collaborators Law; there is no general war-crimes statute.
- Contested labels are always attributed, and warrants or UN findings are never written as convictions.
