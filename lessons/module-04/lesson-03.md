# 04.3 · Reasonableness and Proportionality in Administrative Decisions

A decision can be within the power and based on relevant considerations, and still be one that no reasonable authority would make, or harm a right far more than it needs to. This lesson teaches the two "substantive" grounds of review — reasonableness and proportionality — together with the honest 2026 status of reasonableness after the 2023 amendment and its annulment, so that you know how much weight each ground can carry in your own audit.

## Why it matters

Reasonableness (עילת הסבירות) is the best-known ground of Israeli administrative law and, since 2023, the most politically contested. You will hear it from every side: that it lets unelected judges replace the government's judgment, or that it is the main barrier against corrupt appointments and arbitrary decisions. An activist needs to know what it actually is, when courts actually use it, and where it stands today — and also that, in practice, it is rarely the only ground a successful challenge relies on.

Proportionality (מידתיות) is the main test whenever a decision limits a basic right: a protest restriction, a demolition, a permit condition, a ban on a gathering. It gives you a structured way to show that a less harmful option existed.

## The law

### Reasonableness: a ground made by the courts

There is no statute that creates the reasonableness ground. It was developed in case law, most famously in *Dapei Zahav v. Broadcasting Authority*, HCJ 389/80 (1980), where Barak J. described unreasonableness as an independent ground; the accepted formulation today is a wrong balance between relevant considerations (Hebrew Wikipedia, "עילת הסבירות"). In *Ressler*, Barak J. wrote that reasonable discretion means "allowing appropriate weight to the various factors" (para. 67, Versa translation), and that there are usually several reasonable options (para. 40). The court does not choose the best one; it intervenes only when the decision falls outside that range. ([Ressler, Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ressler-v-minister-defence))

In the Deri-Pinhasi line of cases (1993) the Court held that a Prime Minister's failure to dismiss a minister under indictment could itself be unreasonable. A later ruling, quoted in the Hebrew Wikipedia summary, puts the principle this way:

> פסילת אדם לכהונה מכח עילות כלליות של המשפט הציבורי עשויה להישען על עקרונות הסבירות, ההגינות, וחובת ההגנה על אמון הציבור בנבחריו.

*Gist:* even where no statute disqualifies an office-holder, general public-law principles — reasonableness, fairness and public trust — can require the end of their term. ([הלכת דרעי-פנחסי, Hebrew Wikipedia](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/הלכת_דרעי-פנחסי))

### The 2023 amendment — annulled

In July 2023 the Knesset added s. 15(ד1) to Basic Law: The Judiciary. Wikisource now shows it struck through, with the note "(תיקון מס׳ 3 בוטל בבג״ץ 5658/23)":

> על אף האמור בחוק־יסוד זה, מי שבידו סמכות שפיטה על פי דין, לרבות בית המשפט העליון בשבתו כבית משפט גבוה לצדק, לא ידון בעניין סבירות ההחלטה של הממשלה, של ראש הממשלה או של שר אחר, ולא ייתן צו בעניין כאמור

*Gist:* no court, including the High Court of Justice, would hear arguments on the reasonableness of decisions of the government, the Prime Minister or a minister — including appointments and decisions not to act. ([חוק־יסוד: השפיטה, s. 15(ד1), annulled](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_15))

On 1 January 2024, in *Movement for Quality Government v. Knesset*, HCJ 5658/23, a panel of all 15 justices annulled the amendment by 8 to 7. According to the official abstract, 12 of the 15 held that the Court may review Basic Laws in extreme cases (some news reports count 13, depending on how one justice's view is read). Three justices in the minority would have kept the amendment but read it narrowly, so that it removed only the "balancing" form of reasonableness and still allowed review of arbitrary decisions. ([official abstract, Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/node/704))

### Proportionality: written into the Basic Law

> אין פוגעים בזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק ההולם את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל, שנועד לתכלית ראויה, ובמידה שאינה עולה על הנדרש או לפי חוק כאמור מכוח הסמכה מפורשת בו.

*Gist:* a limit on a basic right must serve a proper purpose and go no further than necessary ("במידה שאינה עולה על הנדרש"). Courts apply this to administrative decisions as well as to statutes. ([חוק־יסוד: כבוד האדם וחירותו, s. 8](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_8))

## How it works in practice

### Where reasonableness stands in 2026

- **The amendment is void.** Since 1 January 2024 the ground applies as it did before 2023, to all authorities, including the government and ministers. The Israel Democracy Institute's explainer says the extreme-unreasonableness standard is restored with the same scope it had before the amendment. ([IDI, 3.1.2024](https://en.idi.org.il/articles/52235))
- **The threshold is high.** Courts intervene only for *extreme* unreasonableness (חוסר סבירות קיצוני). A decision you think is wrong, or worse than an alternative, is not enough.
- **The dispute continues.** Supporters of the 2023 amendment, including the government, argued that the ground gave the Court too broad a scope to intervene in policy decisions (Times of Israel, 1.1.2024). Critics of the broad doctrine have included former Presidents Landau and Grunis and current Deputy President Sohlberg, who has proposed narrowing it for elected officials and relying more on proportionality. Defenders, among them former Justice Zamir and the IDI, say it is used with restraint and mainly guards against improper appointments and decisions (Hebrew Wikipedia, "עילת הסבירות").
- **Legislation.** As of 12 October 2026, no new bill to abolish or limit reasonableness review was listed on the Israel Policy Forum's judicial legislation tracker (an advocacy organisation's tracker; it does list pending bills on the Attorney General and ministry legal advisers). Check [references/laws-index.md](../../references/laws-index.md) and the news before relying on this lesson: a new Knesset could legislate again.
- **In practice.** Recent high-profile rulings against government decisions have tended to rest on several grounds together. In May 2025, for example, the Court's 2–1 ruling on the dismissal of the Shin Bet head relied on the failure to consult the senior appointments advisory committee, the lack of a hearing, a conflict of interest and a thin factual basis (lesson 04.4).

**What this means for you.** Use reasonableness as a supporting ground, framed concretely: "the decision gave no weight to X, which the law makes central" or "the decision ignored the data in Y". Lead with authority, considerations, procedure or proportionality whenever your facts allow.

### Proportionality step by step

Use the tests in order. Each "no" ends the analysis against the authority. (Lesson 03.3 in Module 3 works through the same tests for statutes.)

1. **Proper purpose.** What is the decision for? Is that purpose legitimate under the enabling law?
2. **Rational connection.** Does the measure actually advance the purpose?
3. **Least harmful means.** Is there another measure that achieves the purpose about as well, with less harm? This is where most challenges succeed: propose a concrete alternative.
4. **Proportionality in the narrow sense.** Even if no gentler measure exists, is the benefit worth the harm to the right?

The leading example of proportionality applied to an administrative decision is *Beit Sourik Village Council v. Government of Israel*, HCJ 2056/04 (30 June 2004). The Court accepted the military commander's security purpose for the separation barrier, but held that parts of the route harmed local residents more than an available alternative route would, and annulled those orders. The ruling is relevant here for its method; its context belongs to Module 15. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/beit-sourik-village-council-v-government-israel))

### Reasonableness or proportionality?

| If the decision… | Lead with |
|---|---|
| limits a basic right (expression, property, movement, religion, equality) | Proportionality, with a concrete less harmful alternative |
| ignores data or a consideration the law makes central | Considerations (04.2), then reasonableness |
| is an appointment, dismissal or failure to act | Authority, procedure and conflict of interest (04.4), then reasonableness |
| is a policy choice you disagree with, but within the law | Probably neither: use the political process (Module 12) |

## Worked example

**Situation.** A concrete-mixing plant operates 150 metres from a kindergarten in a mixed Jewish-Arab town. The local planning and building committee extends its temporary use permit (שימוש חורג) for five more years, without conditions. Parents had submitted, during the objection period, air-quality measurements from the Ministry of Environmental Protection's monitoring station showing repeated exceedances of particulate standards during working hours. The committee's decision does not mention the measurements. Hadeel and Ronit, two mothers, audit the decision.

1. **Authority (04.1).** The committee has the power to approve temporary use under the Planning and Building Law. No problem here.
2. **Considerations (04.2).** Public health and environmental impact are relevant under the planning law's purposes; so is the plant's economic value. The measurements were before the committee.
3. **Reasonableness.** The minutes show no discussion of the health data. The parents frame it narrowly: "The committee gave no weight at all to the health data before it, although health is a central planning consideration. A five-year extension without conditions, in the face of that data, falls outside the range of reasonable decisions."
4. **Proportionality-style alternative.** Even though no basic right is directly limited, the parents use the same logic to make the remedy concrete: a shorter extension, working-hour limits during kindergarten hours, dust-control conditions and an obligation to install a monitor. This shows the committee had less harmful options that would still let the plant operate.
5. **Next step.** They file an appeal (ערר) to the district appeals committee within the deadline the Planning and Building Law sets (Module 10 and [current numbers](../../references/current-numbers.md)), rather than going straight to court — the planning route must be used first (lesson 04.5; Module 10).

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your decision audit from lessons 04.1–04.2 (sections A and B of the [decision audit template](../../templates/decision-audit.md)).
- 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write which relevant considerations the decision weighed and roughly what weight it gave each one (none / little / heavy).
2. Write, in one sentence, what makes the result unreasonable — or why it is probably within the range. Be honest: most decisions are.
3. Check whether the decision limits a basic right. If not, skip to step 5.
4. Apply the four proportionality tests. For test 3, write at least one concrete less harmful alternative and explain why it would achieve the purpose about as well.
5. Use the table above to choose which ground to lead with.
6. Fill section C of the template.
7. Write one paragraph, in neutral language, explaining the current status of reasonableness review for a reader who supports the 2023 amendment and one who opposes it. Both should accept it as fair.

### Template

[Decision audit](../../templates/decision-audit.md), section C.

### How you know it worked

Your reasonableness argument names a specific consideration and the weight it was (not) given, rather than saying the decision is "unreasonable" in general. If a right is limited, you have proposed a realistic alternative measure. Your status paragraph attributes each position to its source.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calling a decision "unreasonable" because you disagree with it | The court applies the range of reasonableness and dismisses | Identify a specific consideration that got no or grossly wrong weight | Ask "would any reasonable authority decide this?", not "is it the best?" |
| Telling readers reasonableness was abolished | You give wrong legal information | Correct it: the amendment was annulled on 1.1.2024 | Check the current status before writing |
| Presenting the reasonableness debate as one-sided | You lose readers who disagree, and the course's neutrality | Attribute each position to a named source | Quote both supporters and critics |
| Skipping the "least harmful means" test | Your proportionality argument stays abstract | Propose a concrete alternative measure | Always write at least one alternative |
| Relying on reasonableness alone | A weak case at a high threshold | Add authority, considerations or procedure grounds | Build the full audit first, then choose |

## Review

- Reasonableness asks whether the authority gave proper weight to relevant considerations; courts intervene only for extreme unreasonableness.
- The 2023 amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary (s. 15(ד1)) was annulled by HCJ 5658/23 on 1 January 2024 (8–7); the ground applies as before, and the debate continues.
- Proportionality applies when a decision limits a right: proper purpose, rational connection, least harmful means, benefit against harm.
- Propose a concrete less harmful alternative; it is usually the strongest proportionality argument.
- Use reasonableness as a supporting ground and lead with authority, considerations, procedure or proportionality where you can.
