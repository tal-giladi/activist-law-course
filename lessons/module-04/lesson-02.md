# 04.2 · Discretion: Relevant and Extraneous Considerations

Most powers leave the authority a choice: it "may" grant, refuse, approve or set conditions. That choice is called discretion, and it is never free. In this lesson you learn to read the purpose of a law, list the considerations it makes relevant, and find the extraneous ones — the political, personal or outside pressures that make an otherwise lawful power unlawful in use.

## Why it matters

Once you know the authority had the power (lesson 04.1), the next question is how it used it. Here activists often have their best evidence: a council member's post on social media, a minute that says "residents complained about the group's views", a refusal that appears only after a protest. Israeli courts have long held that a decision based on an extraneous consideration (שיקול זר) is unlawful, even if the same decision could have been reached lawfully. And a decision that ignored a consideration the law requires is just as flawed.

This ground also works across the political spectrum. A hall refused because of a group's opinions, a permit delayed because the applicant criticised the mayor, a grant conditioned on a political statement: the same test applies whoever holds the power and whoever is on the receiving end.

## The law

The rules on considerations come mostly from case law. The court's power to enforce them comes from the Basic Law:

> לתת צווים לרשויות המדינה, לרשויות מקומיות, לפקידיהן ולגופים ולאנשים אחרים הממלאים תפקידים ציבוריים על פי דין, לעשות מעשה או להימנע מעשות מעשה במילוי תפקידיהם כדין

*Gist:* the High Court of Justice may order any public body or official to act, or not to act, so that they carry out their functions lawfully. ([חוק־יסוד: השפיטה, s. 15(ד)(2)](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_השפיטה#סעיף_15))

> כל רשות מרשויות השלטון חייבת לכבד את הזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה.

*Gist:* respecting basic rights, such as dignity and equality, is always a consideration every authority must weigh. ([חוק־יסוד: כבוד האדם וחירותו, s. 11](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו#סעיף_11))

The leading statement of the test is in *Ressler v. Minister of Defence*, HCJ 910/86 (Barak J., para. 64, in the Versa English translation). The Minister's power to defer service "for other reasons" does not let him weigh whatever seems right to him; as Barak J. put it, "The test of their relevance is objective" — it is set by the purpose of the law. ([Ressler, Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ressler-v-minister-defence))

*Gist:* whether a consideration is relevant depends on the purpose of the enabling law, not on what the official happens to think.

## How it works in practice

### Step 1 — Find the purpose

Read the enabling law (lesson 04.1) and ask what it is for. Sources, in order:

1. The section itself and its definitions.
2. The law's purpose section, if it has one ("מטרת חוק זה…").
3. The structure of the law: what other sections deal with.
4. The explanatory notes to the bill (דברי הסבר) on the Knesset legislation database (lesson 02.1).
5. Rulings that interpreted this section (lesson 02.2).
6. General values that apply to every power: equality, basic rights, the public interest, integrity.

### Step 2 — List the considerations

Make a table: every consideration the authority used or should have used, and whether the purpose makes it relevant. A licensing law about safety makes safety, crowd size and fire exits relevant; it does not make the applicant's opinions relevant.

### Step 3 — Find the evidence

Authorities rarely write "we refused because we disagree with them". Look for:

- the reasons in the decision letter (ask for them under Decisions and Reasons Law s. 2א, lesson 04.4);
- minutes (פרוטוקול) of the committee or council, which you can request under the FOI Law (lesson 05.2);
- public statements by the decision-makers;
- timing (a refusal right after a protest or a critical article);
- comparison: how did the authority treat similar applicants? Unequal treatment for no relevant reason is discrimination.

The authority enjoys a presumption of regularity, so the burden is on you to show the flaw. Evidence, not suspicion, moves a court.

### Step 4 — Mixed motives

Often a decision rests on both relevant and extraneous considerations. Courts then ask how much the extraneous consideration influenced the result. If it was decisive or dominant, the decision falls. If the relevant considerations would clearly have led to the same result on their own, the court may let it stand. Your audit should therefore show both what was extraneous and how much it mattered.

### Step 5 — Fettering and dictation

Two related flaws:

- **Fettering (כבילה).** A policy is lawful and useful, but an authority must still be willing to consider exceptions. "We never allow events on Saturdays, no exceptions" may be unlawful fettering if the law expects case-by-case discretion.
- **Dictation (הכתבה).** The holder of the power must decide personally. If a planning committee simply follows a minister's phone call, or a clerk follows a council member's instruction without independent judgment, the discretion was not exercised.

## Worked example

**Situation.** A religious-Zionist youth movement books a municipal community hall for a public lecture by a rabbi whose views on territorial questions are controversial. Two days later the booking is cancelled. The cancellation letter says only: "בשל פניות תושבים". Yair, a parent and one of the organisers, decides to audit the decision. (The same analysis would apply to a left-wing group, a Reform congregation or an Arab cultural association.)

1. **Authority (04.1).** The municipality manages its halls under its own hall-use rules, adopted under its general powers; the culture director signs bookings. Authority is not the problem.
2. **Purpose.** The hall rules exist to share a public asset fairly and to keep events safe: capacity, security, cleaning, opening hours.
3. **Considerations table.**

   | Consideration | Relevant? | Why |
   |---|---|---|
   | Capacity and fire safety | Yes | Purpose of the rules |
   | Need for security arrangements | Yes, if based on concrete information | Public safety |
   | Residents' objection to the speaker's views | No | Opinions are not a ground under the hall rules; expression must be respected (Basic Law s. 11) |
   | Equal access to a public asset | Yes | Equality applies to every authority |

4. **Evidence.** Yair asks in writing for the reasons and for the residents' complaints (in redacted form). The reply attaches three emails objecting to "the speaker's political opinions". No police or security assessment is mentioned. Yair also finds that the same hall hosted a lecture by a speaker with the opposite views a month earlier.
5. **Conclusion for the audit.** "The cancellation appears to rest on an extraneous consideration (objection to the speaker's views) and treats the group unequally. No relevant consideration was cited."
6. **Next step.** A letter to the culture director and the municipal legal adviser asking that the booking be restored within seven days, offering to coordinate security arrangements if the municipality has concrete safety information. The offer matters: it shows the group accepts the relevant consideration while rejecting the extraneous one.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The decision you used in lesson 04.1, with section A of the [decision audit template](../../templates/decision-audit.md) filled in.
- The enabling law, and if possible its explanatory notes on the Knesset database.
- 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. Write the purpose of the enabling law in two lines, citing where you found it.
2. List every consideration the authority stated in the decision.
3. Add the considerations it should have weighed but did not mention.
4. Mark each one relevant or extraneous, with a one-line reason tied to the purpose.
5. For each extraneous consideration, write what evidence shows it played a part. If you have none, write what you would request (reasons, minutes, comparative data) and from whom.
6. Check for fettering (a rule with no exceptions) and dictation (someone else decided).
7. Fill section B of the template and write a two-sentence conclusion.

### Template

[Decision audit](../../templates/decision-audit.md), section B.

### How you know it worked

Every consideration in your table is linked to the purpose of the law by one sentence. For each claim that a consideration was extraneous, you can point to a document, statement, timing or comparison — or you have listed exactly what you will ask for to find out.

- [ ] My conclusion would read the same whichever side of politics the affected group was on.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alleging a political motive with no evidence | The presumption of regularity wins; your credibility drops | Ask for reasons, minutes and comparative data first | Collect evidence before you allege bad motive |
| Calling any consideration you dislike "extraneous" | The authority shows it is in the law's purpose | Tie every claim to the law's purpose | Write the purpose first, then judge considerations |
| Ignoring the relevant considerations the authority did have | The court finds the decision would stand anyway | Show the extraneous one was decisive, or that relevant ones were missing | Address mixed motives directly |
| Attacking a policy as such | Courts accept policies as lawful tools | Argue that the policy left no room for your exceptional case | Ask for an exception and document the refusal |
| Arguing only for "our side" | Readers see a political, not legal, claim | Show the same rule would protect the other side | Test your argument on a group you disagree with |

## Review

- Discretion is a choice within a power, and it must be exercised for the purposes of the law.
- Relevance is objective: it is set by the law's purpose, not the official's views.
- A decision based on a decisive extraneous consideration is unlawful; so is one that ignored a required consideration.
- Evidence comes from reasons, minutes, statements, timing and comparison with similar cases.
- Rigid rules with no exceptions (fettering) and decisions taken on someone else's instruction (dictation) are flaws too.
