# 03.3 · Proportionality Step by Step

Proportionality is the engine of the limitation clause: the words "to an extent no greater than necessary" became a set of tests that decide most constitutional cases in Israel. Here you take the tests apart for laws and regulations (lesson [04.3](../module-04/lesson-03.md) later applies them briefly to administrative decisions), see which test real measures failed and why, and finish by running the full analysis on the measure you started in lesson 03.2.

## Why it matters

Almost nobody argues that a law has no legitimate purpose; the real fight is over whether it goes further than it needs to. Proportionality gives you a structure for that fight, and it is the same structure the Knesset's legal advisers, the ministry's lawyers and the judges use. If your letter or position paper follows it, they can engage with you on their own terms. If it says only "this is excessive", they cannot.

## The law

> אין פוגעים בזכויות שלפי חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק ההולם את ערכיה של מדינת ישראל, שנועד לתכלית ראויה, ובמידה שאינה עולה על הנדרש או לפי חוק כאמור מכוח הסמכה מפורשת בו.

*Gist:* the last two conditions — "for a proper purpose" and "to an extent no greater than necessary" — are where proportionality lives (Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, s. 8; Freedom of Occupation s. 4 uses the same words). ([Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו))

The words "no greater than necessary" were turned into three sub-tests by the Supreme Court, beginning in the late 1990s. In *Tzemach* (1999) the Court quoted its earlier ruling in the investment managers' case: "The first secondary test is the test of suitability or rational connection" and "The second secondary test is the test of the least violative means"; the third weighs benefit against harm. The Court also admitted the limits of the exercise: "Because we cannot weigh, we must estimate" (para. 31). ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/node/386))

## How it works in practice

### Test 0: proper purpose

The purpose must protect a right of others or an important public interest, and the measure must really be aimed at it. Courts rarely strike a law down here. Your job at this stage is to pin the purpose down precisely, from the bill's explanatory notes (דברי הסבר) or the committee minutes, because every later test measures the law against that exact purpose.

### Test 1: rational connection

Does the measure actually advance the purpose? It need not be the only or the best way, but there must be a real, not speculative, link.

- **Evidence:** studies, data from the ministry, the experience of other countries, the authority's own reports.
- **Under-inclusiveness** (the law misses most of the problem) and **over-inclusiveness** (it hits many people who are not part of the problem) are signs the link is weak.
- **Example:** in *Ressler v. Knesset* (2012) the majority held that the Tal Law on deferring military service for yeshiva students lacked a real potential to achieve its own purposes of gradual integration into service and work. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ressler-v-knesset))

### Test 2: least harmful means

Is there another measure that achieves the purpose to about the same degree with less harm to the right? The alternative must be realistic and must achieve the purpose almost as well; a gentler measure that achieves much less does not count.

- **Example:** in *Tzemach* the question was whether military arrest without a judge for up to 96 hours was needed, or whether a shorter period could serve the army's needs. The Court held the 96-hour provision void and suspended the declaration for six months so the Knesset could legislate (paras. 42, 44).
- **Your job:** propose concrete alternatives — a shorter period, a narrower group, an exception, a time limit, a review mechanism, a hearing before the measure applies.

### Test 3: proportionality in the narrow sense

Even if no gentler measure exists, is the extra benefit of this measure worth the extra harm it causes? Compare the **marginal** benefit (what the measure adds over the next-best option) with the **marginal** harm to the right.

- **Example:** in *Academic Center of Law and Business v. Minister of Finance* (2009) the Court, 8–1, struck down the law allowing a privately run prison. The main purpose was to save state money; the majority held that the additional harm to prisoners' liberty and dignity from handing the power to imprison to a profit-making company was disproportionately greater than that benefit. Justice Levy dissented, arguing that the law should be tested in practice first. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/academic-center-law-and-business-v-minister-finance))

### The margin of proportionality

Courts do not ask whether they would have chosen the same measure. The legislature has a margin (מתחם המידתיות); the court intervenes only when the measure falls outside it. In *Gaza Coast Regional Council v. Knesset* (HCJ 1661/05, 9 June 2005), a petition by residents of Gaza settlements and others against the disengagement law, the Court upheld the law 10–1 but struck down four provisions of its compensation scheme and sent them back to the Knesset. (JURIST, 9.6.2005; the Hebrew judgment is on the Supreme Court site.) That pattern — the core survives, a part fails — is common.

### Which test is usually decisive?

| If the measure… | Look hardest at |
|---|---|
| rests on a guess, not data | Rational connection |
| applies to everyone when the problem is a small group | Rational connection (over-inclusive) and least harmful means |
| is permanent, automatic or blanket | Least harmful means: time limits, exceptions, individual review |
| saves money or effort at a heavy cost to a core right | Narrow sense |
| is narrow, temporary and well supported | Probably within the margin — say so honestly |

## Worked example

**Situation.** A bill (invented for this example) would ban any demonstration using amplification within 300 metres of the private home of an elected official, a judge or a senior civil servant, at any hour. Protesters from opposite camps have demonstrated outside ministers' homes and outside the homes of judges and the Attorney General. Yossi, from a group that protests outside a minister's home, and Miriam, from a group that protests outside a judge's home, both want to send comments to the Knesset committee. They use the same analysis.

1. **Right and infringement (03.2).** Freedom of expression and protest, derived from dignity (03.4). The bill bans a form of protest at a place chosen for its message.
2. **Proper purpose.** The explanatory notes cite the privacy and safety of officials' families and neighbours. Proper — they say so.
3. **Rational connection.** Yes: banning amplified protest near homes reduces noise and pressure there.
4. **Least harmful means.** Alternatives that protect neighbours almost as well:
   - quiet hours at night instead of a 24-hour ban;
   - a volume limit instead of a ban on amplification;
   - a 50-metre buffer instead of 300 metres;
   - police powers that already exist against threats and harassment.
5. **Narrow sense.** The marginal gain of a 24-hour, 300-metre ban over quiet hours plus a volume limit is small; the marginal harm — removing protest from the one place where its message is aimed — is large.
6. **Conclusion and request.** They ask the committee to replace the ban with night-time quiet hours and a volume limit, and to apply the rule equally to every official. Both letters are signed in their own names.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your template from lesson 03.2, sections 0–3 filled in.
- The measure's explanatory notes or the decision's reasons, and any data on its effect.
- About 60 minutes.

### Steps

1. Rewrite the purpose in one precise sentence, with its source (section 3).
2. Rational connection (section 4): write the evidence that the measure advances that purpose. Note whether it is over- or under-inclusive, with an example of each if you can.
3. Least harmful means (section 5): write at least two concrete alternatives in the table. For each, say honestly whether it achieves the purpose almost as well.
4. Narrow sense (section 6): write the marginal benefit of the measure over your best alternative, and the marginal harm to the right. Conclude in one sentence.
5. Use the "Which test is usually decisive?" table to name the test your measure is most likely to fail. If it probably passes all of them, write that.
6. Write the strongest argument for the measure, as its supporters would put it (section 8).

### How you know it worked

Each test has its own short paragraph, in order. Your alternatives are specific enough that a ministry lawyer could draft them. Your narrow-sense comparison is between the measure and your best alternative, not between the measure and doing nothing. You have written the other side's best argument fairly.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attacking the purpose when it is plainly legitimate | You lose credibility on the tests that matter | Concede the purpose and move on | Ask "would a court call this purpose improper?" |
| Proposing an alternative that achieves much less | The reader dismisses it as not a real alternative | Find an option that achieves nearly as much | Test each alternative against the purpose |
| Comparing the measure with doing nothing in the narrow-sense test | Almost any measure seems worth it | Compare with your best alternative | Use marginal benefit and marginal harm |
| Skipping evidence at the rational-connection stage | Your claim is an opinion | Find data, reports or examples | Ask the authority for its data (FOI, Module 5) |
| Expecting the court to choose the best measure | Disappointment; the margin of proportionality applies | Show the measure is outside the margin | Argue "outside the range", not "not ideal" |

## Review

- Proportionality turns "no greater than necessary" into tests used in order: proper purpose, rational connection, least harmful means, narrow sense.
- Over- and under-inclusiveness are evidence of a weak link and of less harmful alternatives.
- A real alternative achieves the purpose almost as well with less harm; propose at least two.
- The narrow-sense test compares marginal benefit with marginal harm (*Academic Center*, 2009).
- Courts leave the legislature a margin; often the core of a law survives and a part fails (*Gaza Coast*, 2005).
