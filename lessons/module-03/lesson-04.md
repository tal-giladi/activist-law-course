# 03.4 · Rights the Courts Derived: Equality, Expression, Religion, Protest

The rights activists invoke most — equality, free speech, freedom of religion and from religion, the right to demonstrate — appear in no Basic Law by name. The courts protected them first as basic principles of the legal system and, after 1992, as parts of human dignity. In this lesson you learn how that derivation works, the leading ruling and test for each right, and you finish by writing the stage-one paragraph of a constitutional argument for the derived right your cause depends on.

## Why it matters

If you write "this violates my freedom of expression" and the reader asks "under which Basic Law?", you need an answer. The answer is a ruling, not a section number, and it comes with a test. Knowing the test tells you what facts to collect: for a protest ban, the probability of harm; for a religious arrangement, whether anyone is coerced; for equality, whether people in the same situation are treated differently. Modules 6 and 9 go deeper into protest and anti-discrimination law; this lesson gives you the constitutional base for both.

## The law

### The Declaration of Independence

> תקיים שויון זכויות חברתי ומדיני גמור לכל אזרחיה בלי הבדל דת, גזע ומין; תבטיח חופש דת, מצפון, לשון, חינוך ותרבות;

*Gist:* the state will ensure complete equality of social and political rights for all its citizens regardless of religion, race or sex, and will guarantee freedom of religion, conscience, language, education and culture. The Declaration is not itself a law that can void a statute, but Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty s. 1 says rights are to be honoured "ברוח העקרונות שבהכרזה". ([מגילת העצמאות](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/מגילת_העצמאות_של_מדינת_ישראל))

### Dignity as the source

> אין פוגעים בחייו, בגופו, או בכבודו של אדם באשר הוא אדם.

*Gist:* no one may harm a person's dignity as such (s. 2). Since 1992 the Supreme Court has read "dignity" to include those parts of equality, expression and religious freedom that are closely tied to a person's dignity — not every possible aspect of them. ([Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_כבוד_האדם_וחירותו))

### The Nation-State Basic Law and equality

> מימוש הזכות להגדרה עצמית לאומית במדינת ישראל ייחודי לעם היהודי.

*Gist:* the exercise of the right to national self-determination in Israel is unique to the Jewish people (Basic Law: Israel – the Nation-State of the Jewish People, s. 1(ג)). Section 7 says the state sees developing Jewish settlement as a national value. The law does not mention equality. In *Hasson v. Knesset* (HCJ 5555/18, 8 July 2021), an 11-justice panel rejected the petitions 10–1. President Hayut held that the law does not negate Israel's character as a Jewish and democratic state and that the right to equality, though not written in a Basic Law, remains protected by the case law. Justice Karra, dissenting, would have struck down ss. 1(ג), 4 and 7, holding that the silence on equality and minority rights was deliberate. ([Wikisource](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_ישראל_–_מדינת_הלאום_של_העם_היהודי); [Hebrew Wikipedia summary](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/בג"ץ_חוק_הלאום))

## How it works in practice

### Equality

**The test:** people who are alike in the relevant respect must be treated alike; a distinction based on an irrelevant difference is discrimination.

| Ruling | Facts | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| *Shakdiel* (HCJ 153/87, 1988) | An observant woman nominated to a local religious council was excluded because she is a woman | Gender was an irrelevant distinction for an administrative body; the exclusion was unlawful |
| *Miller* (HCJ 4541/94, 1995) | The air force refused to train women as pilots, citing cost and planning | 3–2: budget and planning considerations did not justify a blanket exclusion of women |
| *Ka'adan* (HCJ 6698/95, 2000) | An Arab couple was refused a home in Katzir, built on state land allocated via the Jewish Agency | The state may not discriminate on nationality or religion in allocating land, directly or through others; the state was told to reconsider their request on an equal basis |
| *Ressler v. Knesset* (HCJ 6298/07, 2012) | The Tal Law deferred military service for yeshiva students | 6–3: the law violated equality as part of dignity and failed proportionality |

Equality claims come from every direction: women in religious institutions, Arab citizens in land allocation, secular and religious Israelis on the burden of military service.

### Freedom of expression

**The test:** speech may be limited to protect public order or other rights only where there is a near certainty (or, in later rulings, a high probability) of serious harm. A bad tendency is not enough. This comes from *Kol Ha'am* (1953), which you read in lesson 01.6. Expression is now treated as part of dignity, and limits on it are tested under the limitation clause. Module 6 deals with incitement, defamation and online speech.

### Freedom of religion and from religion

**The test:** religious feelings are a legitimate consideration for an authority, but they do not justify religious coercion; they are balanced against other rights.

- *Horev v. Minister of Transportation* (HCJ 5016/96, 1997): the minister closed Jerusalem's Bar-Ilan Street to traffic during prayer hours on Sabbaths. The Court held that respecting the observant residents' way of life is a legitimate consideration, balanced against drivers' freedom of movement, and found that the decision had not adequately weighed the interests of secular residents. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/horev-v-minister-transportation))
- *Ragen v. Ministry of Transport* (HCJ 746/07, 2011): on "mehadrin" bus lines, women were told to sit at the back. The Court held that coerced separation is unlawful and that operators may not direct women where to sit; passengers may choose to separate voluntarily, without pressure on others. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/ragen-v-ministry-transport))

The question that decides most cases is: **voluntary or coerced?**

### Freedom of demonstration

**The test:** the police must first try to protect a lawful demonstration; a ban is the last resort and needs a high probability of serious harm that physical measures cannot prevent.

- *Levi v. Southern District Police Commander* (HCJ 153/83, 1984): after a protester was killed at a Peace Now march, the police refused a licence for a memorial march on the same route. The Court held that "conjectures, speculations and apprehensions" were not enough and ordered the licence granted. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/node/583))
- *Majority Camp v. Israel Police* (HCJ 2557/05, 2006): organisers of a march supporting the Gaza disengagement were told to pay for security. The Court held the police may not shift the cost of security and public order onto demonstrators, which would put a price tag on a constitutional right. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/majority-camp-v-israel-police))

The same tests protect demonstrations against a government and in favour of it. Permits under the Police Ordinance are taught in Module 6.

## Worked example

**Situation.** A city's culture department plans a free concert in a public park for the Haredi community, with separate seating areas for men and women at the community's request. A women's rights group says this is unlawful segregation in public space. A Haredi residents' committee says it is the only way its members can attend. Both write to the city's legal adviser, and the city asks Dana, a volunteer with a neutral civic forum, for a one-page analysis.

1. **Which rights?** Equality and dignity of women (derived from dignity; *Shakdiel*, *Ragen*); freedom of religion and culture of the Haredi residents (derived; *Horev*: religious feelings are a legitimate consideration).
2. **The deciding question: voluntary or coerced?** Under *Ragen*, an arrangement that people freely choose is lawful; one that tells people where they must sit, or lets others pressure them, is not.
3. **Facts to check.** Will there be a mixed or open area for anyone who wants it? Who enforces the seating — ushers, signs, or nobody? Is public money used, and is the event open to all residents?
4. **Proportionality-style options (03.3).** Separate areas plus a clearly marked open area; no ushers directing women; signs that say seating is voluntary; equal quality of seating.
5. **Dana's conclusion.** "A concert with separate sections that residents may choose, plus an open section, no enforcement and equal conditions, is likely to be consistent with *Ragen*. Directing women to a section, or enforcing separation, is likely to be unlawful." She attributes the women's group's and the committee's positions and does not take a side on the policy.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- Your proportionality analysis from lessons 03.2–03.3.
- The [ruling brief template](../../templates/ruling-brief.md) from lesson 01.6.
- About 50 minutes.

### Steps

1. Decide whether the right in your analysis is written in a Basic Law or derived. If derived, name it.
2. From the tables above, pick the leading ruling for that right. If none fits, search Versa or the Supreme Court site for a ruling on your issue.
3. Open the ruling and read the abstract. Fill a ruling brief: question, holding, test, result.
4. Write the stage-one paragraph of your argument (four to six sentences): the right, its source (dignity, s. 2, as interpreted in the ruling), the test, and the facts that show the infringement.
5. Add one sentence on how the same test would protect a group on the other side of your issue.
6. Paste the paragraph into section 1 of your proportionality analysis.

### Template

[Ruling brief](../../templates/ruling-brief.md) for the ruling, and section 1 of the [proportionality analysis](../../templates/proportionality-analysis.md) for the paragraph.

### How you know it worked

Your paragraph names the right, the Basic Law section it is derived from, the ruling and its test, and at least two facts. A reader who disagrees with your cause could still accept the paragraph as an accurate statement of the law.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citing "the Basic Law on equality" | There is none; the reader doubts the rest | Cite dignity (s. 2) as interpreted in a named ruling | Check whether a right is written or derived |
| Claiming every aspect of equality or speech is constitutional | Courts protect only the parts closely tied to dignity | Show why this case touches the core of dignity | Use facts that show humiliation, exclusion or silencing |
| Treating religious feelings as a trump card, or as irrelevant | The argument ignores the balance the courts strike | Weigh them against the other right | Ask "voluntary or coerced?" |
| Arguing that police "may ban any protest that might turn violent" | Contradicts *Levi*; the ban is a last resort | Ask what physical protection was considered | Ask for the police's risk assessment |
| Using a ruling only when it suits your side | Readers on the other side dismiss you | Show the same test protects them | Add one "other side" sentence |

## Review

- Equality, expression, religion and protest are not named in a Basic Law; the courts protect them as basic principles and, since 1992, as parts of dignity.
- Equality: alike treated alike (*Shakdiel*, *Miller*, *Ka'adan*, *Ressler v. Knesset*); *Hasson* held that the Nation-State Law leaves the case-law right to equality in place, over a dissent.
- Expression: limits need a near certainty or high probability of serious harm (*Kol Ha'am*).
- Religion: religious feelings count, coercion does not (*Horev*, *Ragen*).
- Protest: protect first, ban last; no price tag on the right (*Levi*, *Majority Camp*).
