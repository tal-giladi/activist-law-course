# 03.1 · A Constitution Built from Basic Laws

Israel has no single document called "the Constitution". It has thirteen Basic Laws, written one at a time since 1958, which the Supreme Court treats as a constitution in the making. In this lesson you learn where that arrangement came from, how to tell which provisions are protected by a special majority, why the courts say Basic Laws outrank ordinary laws and why others disagree, and you finish with a fact sheet on the constitutional right behind your own cause.

## Why it matters

When you write "this law is unconstitutional" in a letter, a position paper or a petition, the reader will ask: which constitution? You need to name a Basic Law and a section, know whether that section can be changed by an ordinary majority, and know which ruling says courts may enforce it. Lesson 01.3 put Basic Laws at the top of the hierarchy. This lesson explains how they got there and why that place is still argued about, so that you can use them with precision and present the debate fairly.

## The law

### The Harari decision (13 June 1950)

> החוקה תהיה בנויה פרקים פרקים, באופן שכל אחד מהם יהווה חוק יסודי בפני עצמו.

*Gist:* the First Knesset tasked its Constitution, Law and Justice Committee with drafting a constitution, to be built chapter by chapter, each chapter a Basic Law of its own; together they would form the constitution. ([החלטת הררי, Hebrew Wikipedia](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/החלטת_הררי))

The decision did not say when the chapters would be finished, how they would be adopted, or whether they would outrank ordinary laws. Those gaps are the root of most of the disputes in this module.

### Formal entrenchment: the Basic Law decides who may change it

> הכנסת תיבחר בבחירות כלליות, ארציות, ישירות, שוות, חשאיות ויחסיות, לפי חוק הבחירות לכנסת; אין לשנות סעיף זה, אלא ברוב של חברי הכנסת.

*Gist:* Knesset elections must be general, national, direct, equal, secret and proportional, and this section may be changed only by a majority of all MKs (Basic Law: The Knesset, s. 4). Section 46 adds that the special majority is needed in all three readings, for an express or an implied change. ([חוק־יסוד: הכנסת](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_הכנסת))

> אין לשנות חוק־יסוד זה אלא בחוק־יסוד שנתקבל ברוב של חברי הכנסת.

*Gist:* Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation may be changed only by another Basic Law passed by a majority of all MKs (s. 7). ([חוק־יסוד: חופש העיסוק](https://he.wikisource.org/wiki/חוק-יסוד:_חופש_העיסוק))

Most Basic Laws, including Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, contain no such clause. That point comes back in lesson 03.5.

## How it works in practice

### The thirteen Basic Laws in force (October 2026)

| Year first passed | Basic Law | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 1958 | The Knesset | Elections, the Knesset's term and powers |
| 1960 | Israel Lands | State land may not be sold, with exceptions |
| 1964 | The President of the State | The President |
| 1968 (re-enacted 1992, 2001) | The Government | Forming and running the government |
| 1975 | The State Economy | Taxes and payments need a law (lesson 04.1 uses it) |
| 1976 | The Military | The army is subject to the government |
| 1980 | Jerusalem, the Capital of Israel | Status of Jerusalem |
| 1984 | The Judiciary | Courts, judges, the Judicial Selection Committee |
| 1988 | The State Comptroller | The Comptroller and Ombudsman |
| 1992 | Human Dignity and Liberty | Life, body, dignity, property, liberty, privacy, movement |
| 1992 (re-enacted 1994) | Freedom of Occupation | The right to work in any occupation |
| 2014 | Referendum | A referendum before giving up sovereign territory |
| 2018 | Israel – the Nation-State of the Jewish People | National identity, symbols, language, settlement |

The first nine are mostly "institutional": they set up the organs of state. The two 1992 laws are the first that protect individual rights against the Knesset itself. No Basic Law yet sets out how Basic Laws are made or changed, and there is no complete bill of rights: equality, expression and religion are not written in any Basic Law (lesson 03.4).

### Three steps the courts took

1. **Bergman (1969): entrenchment is enforceable.** A 1969 law funded only parties already in the Knesset. It passed without a majority of all MKs. The High Court held that it breached the "equal" elections requirement in the entrenched s. 4 and ordered the Minister of Finance not to act on it unless the Knesset re-enacted it with the required majority or removed the inequality. The Knesset amended the law. This was a narrow, procedural kind of review: the Knesset could still pass anything, with 61 votes. ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/bergman-v-minister-finance))
2. **1992: the rights Basic Laws.** Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty passed 32 to 21; a proposed entrenchment clause failed by one vote (Hebrew Wikipedia, "המהפכה החוקתית"). Both 1992 laws included a limitation clause (lesson 03.2). Professor Claude Klein called this "the quiet constitutional revolution".
3. **Mizrahi Bank (9 November 1995): substantive review.** The Supreme Court held that the Basic Laws have constitutional status above ordinary laws and that a court may declare void a law that breaks a limitation clause. The judges reached that result by different routes:
   - **Barak P.** — the Knesset wears two hats: an ordinary legislator and a *constituent authority* (רשות מכוננת). Basic Laws are made with the second hat, so they bind the first.
   - **Shamgar P. (ret.)** — the Knesset's power is unlimited, including the power to rank its own norms; a law that says it is a Basic Law and limits later laws binds those later laws.
   - **Cheshin J.** — disagreed that the Knesset holds constituent power or can adopt a constitution this way; a constitution needs broader public participation. He accepted that the Knesset can bind itself by an express entrenchment clause.

   ([Versa](https://versa.cardozo.yu.edu/opinions/united-mizrahi-bank-v-migdal-cooperative-village); [Hebrew Wikipedia](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/המהפכה_החוקתית))

### The debate, attributed

- **Supporters** of the 1992–1995 developments — among them Barak, MK Amnon Rubinstein (a sponsor of the 1992 laws) and Professor Yaniv Roznai — say the limitation clause only makes sense if courts can enforce it, and that the Knesset debates show members knew review was possible.
- **Critics** — among them former Supreme Court President Moshe Landau, Professor Ruth Gavison, former MK Michael Eitan and Professor Gideon Sapir — say the laws passed with a small attendance, the public was not told it was adopting a constitution, and the courts, not the Knesset, created their own power to strike down laws.

All of these are named in the Hebrew Wikipedia article. Both sides agree on the text; they disagree on what the Knesset meant and who should have the last word. That disagreement drives lesson 03.5.

## Worked example

**Situation.** A new list, "Reshima Ezrachit" (invented), is running for the Knesset for the first time. A bill is proposed that would give free television airtime only to lists that already sit in the outgoing Knesset. Noa, the list's volunteer coordinator, wants to know whether a constitutional argument exists before she writes to the Constitution Committee.

1. **Find the Basic Law and the section.** Elections: Basic Law: The Knesset, s. 4. The word that matters is "שוות" (equal).
2. **Check entrenchment.** Section 4 ends "אין לשנות סעיף זה, אלא ברוב של חברי הכנסת", and s. 46 requires that majority in every reading, for implied changes too.
3. **Find the leading case.** *Bergman* held that "equal" covers equality of opportunity between lists, not only one person, one vote, and that a law that breaks it needs 61 votes in every reading.
4. **Draw the conclusion.** The bill is not automatically void. But if it passes by less than a majority of all MKs in any reading, it is open to challenge under *Bergman*. Noa's letter asks the committee's legal adviser whether the bill is treated as changing s. 4, and asks the chair to record the vote count in each reading.
5. **Keep it neutral.** The same argument would protect any new list, from any part of the spectrum. Noa's letter says so.

## Do it yourself

### You need

- The cause and Basic Law right you chose in lesson 01.3 (or any right you care about).
- Hebrew Wikisource and the Versa site, about 40 minutes.

### Steps

1. Open the Basic Law that protects your right on Hebrew Wikisource. Copy the section number and its exact Hebrew text.
2. Read the end of that Basic Law. Does it have an entrenchment clause (נוקשות, or "אין לשנות ... אלא ברוב")? Write which majority changes it, or "ordinary majority".
3. Check whether the Basic Law has a limitation clause (lesson 03.2 explains it). Write its section number.
4. Note the year the Basic Law was passed. Is the law you are worried about older? (Lesson 01.3: s. 10 of Human Dignity and Liberty preserves earlier law.)
5. Find one ruling that applied this right. Use the rulings linked in this module or search Versa by keyword.
6. Write two sentences on the judicial review debate: one a supporter would write, one a critic would write, each attributed to a named source from this lesson.
7. Put it all on one page as your constitutional fact sheet. You will reuse it in lessons 03.2–03.4 and in the project.

### Template

No separate template. Use this structure for your fact sheet:

```text
Right:                     (e.g. freedom of occupation)
Basic Law and section:     (name, section number, link)
Exact text:                (Hebrew, copied)
Entrenched?                (yes: majority needed / no)
Limitation clause:         (section number)
Year passed:               (and is my target law older?)
Leading ruling:            (name, case number, year, link, one-line holding)
Debate in two lines:       (supporter: ... — critic: ...)
```

### How you know it worked

Every field is filled from a primary source you opened, with a link. The entrenchment field quotes the Basic Law's own words, not a website's summary. The debate lines name a person or body for each side.

## What goes wrong

| Mistake | Consequence | How to fix | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing "unconstitutional" without naming a Basic Law and section | The reader cannot check your claim and dismisses it | Add the Basic Law, the section and a link | Fill the fact sheet before you write |
| Assuming every Basic Law is entrenched | You claim a special majority was needed when it was not | Read the end of the Basic Law for a נוקשות clause | Quote the clause, or write "not entrenched" |
| Citing Mizrahi Bank as if all judges agreed on the reason | An informed reader corrects you | Attribute the constituent-authority theory to Barak and note the other views | Name the judge with the theory |
| Presenting the constitutional revolution as either settled or illegitimate | You lose readers on the other side | Give one attributed sentence from each side | Use named sources, not adjectives |
| Calling the 2018 Nation-State Law or the 2014 Referendum law "not a Basic Law" | Factually wrong; both were passed as Basic Laws | Distinguish the status of the text from your view of it | Check the Wikisource title |

## Review

- The Harari decision (1950) chose a constitution in chapters; there are thirteen Basic Laws, and no Basic Law on how Basic Laws are made.
- Some sections are formally entrenched (Basic Law: The Knesset s. 4; Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation s. 7); most are not.
- *Bergman* (1969) enforced an entrenched section; *Mizrahi Bank* (1995) held that Basic Laws outrank ordinary laws and allowed substantive review.
- The judges in *Mizrahi Bank* disagreed on why; supporters and critics of the "constitutional revolution" still do. Attribute each view.
